# Frameworks: Keith Rabois

25 frameworks, roughly most-used first, built from the extract notes on 89 sources (his 2014 "How to Operate" lecture, about 85 long talks and interviews from 2013 to 2026, and two chief of staff posts). "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds, not quotes. S001 and S002 are Substack posts his chief of staff wrote from his feedback, so they are secondhand and marked as such.

---

## 1. The spike (top 10 basis points)
- **What it is:** He looks for one trait where a person is in the top 1 to 10 basis points of anyone he has met, or a rare combination of two traits. Without a spike, the odds that someone changes an industry round to zero. For founders it is the main filter. For hires he matches spikes to the company's problems instead of expecting stars in every seat.
- **Parts:** Find the trait early (he says every great founder spiked within the first one to four minutes, S027); check it fits what the company needs: "But they have to have a trait. And it has to match the company." (S033); build around the gaps with complements instead of fixing weaknesses (S012, S060); do not pretend a whole company is spikes.
- **When to use:** Picking a cofounder, a first executive, a CEO, or judging a key hire the founder is excited about.
- **How to score:** 5 if you can name the trait and it lines up with the company's biggest blocker; 3 if the person is strong but balanced with no standout; 1 if "well rounded" or "solid" is the best description. For ordinary hires, score fit to problem, not spike: "any CEO tells you that everybody in their company is like top 10 baseboards is just purely like either stupid or lying" (S084)
- **Quote:** "they're in the top one to 10 basis points in the world on some trait" (S033)
- **Sources:** 20 (S012, S014, S018, S019, S023, S027, S028, S029, S033, S034, S037, S038, S039, S045, S055, S070, S076, S081, S083, S084)

## 2. Undiscovered talent
- **What it is:** A startup cannot win proven people, because big profitable companies will outbid it for them. So it has to find people the recruiting machines cannot score: young, odd, off-spec, or under-used inside your own building. He learned the rule from Peter Thiel in his first week at PayPal (S082) and applies it to every hire and investment since.
- **Parts:** Ask what other people will miss about this person; look in strange places (two or three of his best hires came from his soccer team, S037); poach under-leveraged people already in the company (S064, S085); once the company has scale, hire technical and design interns (S019, S068); accept that you will get false positives and false negatives.
- **When to use:** Any hire, and especially when the founder wants a big-company resume for a role nobody has done before.
- **How to score:** High if the hire has a trait others underprice and will sit in person near leadership; low if the plan is to outbid Google or Meta for a known name on cash. A candidate who has to weigh your offer against the giant's is a warning: "if you have to think about it like you're not the right fit for our company" (S016)
- **Quote:** "you can't build a company successfully with talent that other people know how to assess" (S012)
- **Sources:** 18 (S004, S007, S012, S016, S026, S028, S030, S034, S037, S042, S056, S059, S061, S067, S072, S082, S083, S085)

## 3. Barrels and ammunition
- **What it is:** Most people, even great ones, are ammunition. Barrels are the few who can take an idea from conception to the finish line and bring everyone needed along. The number of barrels caps how many initiatives a company can run at once. Adding ammunition without barrels adds burn and frustration, not output.
- **Parts:** Count your barrels; list the initiatives; cut the initiatives down to the barrel count; hire any barrel on sight; feed barrels with equity, promotion and attention. A barrel works fire and forget and escalates early, with what they tried and the root cause (S007, S020). Barrels are culturally specific, so you have to see them inside your own company (S018, S027).
- **When to use:** Planning after a raise, adding headcount, adding a product line, or deciding who owns an initiative.
- **How to score:** His counts vary by telling, so treat them as a range. PayPal: 12 to 15 (S018), 13 to 16 of 254 (S032), 12 to 17 of 254 (S007), 8 to 15 (S082). A good company has two to 15 (S007); a two to three billion dollar company may have two to four (S032); a healthy company two to five (S018). Engineering: about 1 barrel per 10 to 20 engineers (S006). Score 5 if every live initiative has a named barrel; 1 if initiatives outnumber barrels: "If you have three and you try to do 10 things, you may break everything." (S015)
- **Quote:** "most people, most great people even are actually ammunition. But what you need in your company are barrels." (S006)
- **Sources:** 13 (S006, S007, S012, S013, S014, S015, S018, S020, S027, S032, S055, S056, S082)

## 4. Expand scope until it breaks
- **What it is:** How you find barrels. Give a small, even trivial task. If it is done well, give more. Keep widening scope until the person breaks, then keep them at the level they can hold. Learned from David Sacks at PayPal (S008, S012).
- **Parts:** The smoothie test, a mundane task done unusually well (S006, S056); a stretch ladder each quarter, measuring the quality of performance (S082); watch whose desk people outside the reporting line walk to (S006, S067); on a resume, read for expanding scope of responsibility (S022). Do not overshoot: "if you jump too far ahead of that curve the person will suffocate" (S040)
- **When to use:** Deciding whom to promote, who gets the new initiative, and whether a recent hire is a barrel.
- **How to score:** High if the person has absorbed several scope jumps with quality held and peers seek them out; low if their scope has not changed since they were hired, or they failed without warning anyone.
- **Quote:** "expand the scope of their responsibilities until it breaks. And it will break." (S006)
- **Sources:** 13 (S006, S008, S012, S018, S022, S026, S030, S032, S040, S049, S056, S067, S082)

## 5. Payback and contribution margin
- **What it is:** The money test for any growth spend. How fast acquisition spend comes back, and what you keep after the cost of acquiring the customer. He calls payback his number one criterion when a company buys customers (S028) and keeps a fixed bar that does not move with the market.
- **Parts:** Contribution margin is gross margin minus the cost of acquiring the customer, and should tie to the bank account (S079); judge marketing by payback, never by percent of revenue (S079); optimize contribution dollars, not percentages (S044); the founder masters this personally before there is a CFO (S079); never step on the gas with upside-down unit economics (S074).
- **When to use:** A plan to scale paid acquisition, hire a growth team, or raise to "pour fuel" on a channel.
- **How to score:** His thresholds: payback of six to nine months (S089); contribution margin of 15 percent is good, 20 excellent, 23 world class (S087). Negative contribution margin at scale scores 1 regardless of growth: "it's extremely dangerous to scale a business that has a negative contribution margin" (S079)
- **Quote:** "the time on a CAC to pay back to me is an absolute barometer and my standards haven't changed in 23 years" (S037)
- **Sources:** 14 (S008, S009, S028, S031, S032, S037, S040, S044, S074, S079, S084, S085, S087, S089)

## 6. Cult, concrete, and the first ten
- **What it is:** Every successful company is a cult: a shared secret about the world, a way of working that reinforces it, and hiring that selects for it. Culture is liquid early and sets like concrete, so the first hires decide it. Culture is a decision rule, not a poster.
- **Parts:** State the secret about market, product and people (S055); write values as what you will not do: "values are only meaningful when they say what you're not going to do" (S022); hire the first ten as people you want copied ten times (S064, S072); correct anyone off on values right away (S021); watch the hiring ratio of new hires to current headcount, because when it runs high the newcomers set the culture (S061). Cult dilutes past roughly 500 to 1,000 people (S012, S021). He admits he did not set culture well enough at OpenStore (S021).
- **When to use:** Early hiring plans, a hiring spree after a raise, perks and office decisions, a values document.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can say the secret and the values that exclude, and screened the first ten as copies they would want; low if values are generic or headcount is growing fast enough that new hires outnumber the people who carry the culture.
- **Quote:** "take your first 10 employees and assume each one's going to replicate themselves 10 times" (S072)
- **Sources:** 14 (S006, S012, S021, S022, S029, S032, S055, S059, S061, S064, S070, S072, S084, S086)

## 7. Calendar audit
- **What it is:** Write down your ranked priorities, then compare them with where your time actually went. They almost never match, and recruiting is the usual gap. He has taught it since 2013 and runs it on himself every quarter through a time report from his chief of staff.
- **Parts:** Top three priorities on a whiteboard; open the calendar for the last week or month; measure the fraction that maps; split time into buckets (pitches, one-on-ones, board meetings, recruiting, internal meetings) and look at the ratios and the change from last quarter (S053); protect priorities on the calendar before an assistant fills the rest (S041); keep some scheduling yourself (S026, S030).
- **When to use:** A founder who says hiring or a launch is the top priority, a CEO who feels stretched, or any question of whether to take on a new commitment.
- **How to score:** If recruiting is the number one priority, about 25 percent of time is right (S006). Sunday night check: "more than half your calendar is already booked, you probably need another senior leader." (S061)
- **Quote:** "now open your Google calendar and it's always a Google Calendar and then let's see what fraction of time last week or last month actually maps to those priorities" (S035)
- **Sources:** 12 (S006, S019, S035, S039, S041, S045, S053, S061, S063, S071, S076, S077)

## 8. The business equation and the dashboard
- **What it is:** Every business reduces to an equation of a few variables (sales, marketing, product, pricing, cost). A strong executive knows the weights and which dial to turn when one resists. The founder writes the equation; the dashboard reflects it and goes to everyone.
- **Parts:** Write the equation on a whiteboard: "literally go to whiteboard and write out the business equation and then make the dashboards reflect that" (S014); when one variable resists, find the path of least resistance in another (S008, S082); do not split work so finely that each owner optimizes one variable (S008, S082); push the dashboard down so every person can decide well.
- **When to use:** A team stuck pushing one metric uphill, a reorg, or a decision about what to measure.
- **How to score:** Dashboard test: "what fraction of your employees use that dashboard every day. If it's actually useful, it should be close to 100%." (S006) A founder who cannot say which dial moves the outcome most scores low.
- **Quote:** "what dials have what weights to them and how to toggle different dials to achieve the same output goal" (S012)
- **Sources:** 11 (S006, S008, S012, S014, S025, S026, S030, S031, S038, S053, S082)

## 9. Core risks, a name on each, hardest first *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Write down the three (at most five) things that will kill the company, check that someone world class owns each one, and solve them in order of difficulty, not ease.
- **Parts:** List the risks; for each, does the founding team have an unfair advantage on it, and if not, recruit (S009, S037); name a directly responsible individual per risk (S035, S070); take the hardest first (S054, S068); commit with no hedge, then list the blockers (S024); no backup plan (S064).
- **When to use:** Any plan, raise or reorg. Also when a founder is working on easy, visible problems while a fatal one waits.
- **How to score:** High if each core risk has a named owner who is world class at it and the hardest is being worked now; low if the list is long, unowned, or ordered by what is easiest.
- **Quote:** "Marshaling the right talent against the biggest possible problems, and solving them in the order that's most fatal to you." (S004)
- **Sources:** 11 (S004, S008, S009, S024, S028, S035, S037, S054, S064, S068, S070)

## 10. The interview and reference loop *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Interviews only teach what paper cannot, references do most of the work, and the 30-day check tells you if you were right.
- **Parts:** Read the profile first and only probe the doubts (S020); ask what their value proposition is as a product against similar resumes (S012, S079); ask what they would have done differently as CEO of their last company, then why they could not persuade the real CEO (S007); ask why until answers go mushy, where four levels is typical and five or six exceptional (S012); read their work product (S030); hear a junior colleague's read before giving yours (S026); run many references, across jobs, with skip levels, and do them yourself (S077); 30 days in, ask whether you would make the same hire.
- **When to use:** Any senior hire, and any time a founder wants to hand references to a recruiter.
- **How to score:** He cites 20 references per senior hire as the gold standard and says most founders can do fewer than 10 but must do them personally (S007, S077). The 30-day answer predicts the outcome; he counts only about seven exceptions in 23 years (S064).
- **Quote:** "if you ask yourself 30 days after any hire, would you make the same decision?" (S007)
- **Sources:** 10 (S007, S012, S020, S026, S030, S061, S064, S077, S079, S084)

## 11. Same information, staged transparency
- **What it is:** Bad decisions usually come from missing information. Give people what the CEO has and they decide as the CEO would.
- **Parts:** Stages: metrics first, then board decks shown to every employee after the meeting, then notes for every meeting of more than two people, then glass-walled rooms (S006); share the board deck unedited except the compensation slide (S084); weekly company meeting (S031); public weekly metrics in a turnaround (S015); share goals down to each support rep (S064).
- **When to use:** A team making decisions the founder dislikes, a remote or growing org, a board meeting.
- **How to score:** If people made a bad call without the context the CEO had, it is the CEO's fault: "you can't really get annoyed or furious with your colleagues when they make dumb decisions" (S084)
- **Quote:** "when people have the same information same context they tend to make the same decisions" (S082)
- **Sources:** 10 (S006, S015, S031, S053, S056, S058, S064, S067, S082, S084)

## 12. Look for anomalous data
- **What it is:** The future shows up as data you did not expect. Every person, CEO to intern, should hunt for it. Then use data to test a hypothesis, not to generate one.
- **Parts:** Slice data many ways (S003); notice unexpected adopters, like eBay sellers at PayPal (S025, S083); productize behavior users already invent with friction (S012); follow users, do not ask them, except in enterprise with a few decision makers (S007, S082); change the movie only on an anomalous spark (S064).
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to pivot, what to build next, or whether early traction is real.
- **How to score:** High if the decision rests on an unexpected, repeated signal in usage; low if it rests on surveys or what users say they want.
- **Quote:** "you kind of want to look for the anomalies. You don't actually want to look for the expected behavior." (S006)
- **Sources:** 10 (S003, S004, S006, S008, S012, S025, S054, S064, S082, S083)

## 13. Inputs, not outputs (changed view)
- **What it is:** Judge people on the quality, speed and discipline of their work toward a goal, not on whether the number moved. If you grade only outputs, your best people avoid risky, high-return problems.
- **Older view (2014):** His YC lecture was built on Andy Grove's output focus (S006); he later describes that lecture as output-driven (S059).
- **Newer view (2020 onward, marked current):** "I've shifted to mostly valuing inputs in in looking at the outputs as a byproduct" (S014, 2023). His reason is that outputs push top people to pick only what they already know how to do (S048, S059).
- **Parts:** Grade inputs: font choices on a slide, email speed, slide thought in reviews (S082); reread old memos and judge the analysis, not the result (S052); know whether your company is input-driven or output-driven and hire to match (S088). The chief of staff's post (S001, secondhand) applies this to executive reviews.
- **When to use:** Setting goals or comp for a team on a risky bet, reviewing an executive whose metric missed.
- **How to score:** High if people volunteer for the hardest bets and are reviewed on how they worked; low if every goal is a number and nobody takes the 10x project. He admits the link is weakest in venture (S076).
- **Quote:** "So if for no other reason you need to switch to inputs to get your best people to volunteer for the biggest possible levers." (S048)
- **Sources:** 10 newer (S001 secondhand, S004, S014, S038, S048, S052, S059, S060, S082, S088); older view S006

## 14. Value creation versus value preservation
- **What it is:** Before any hire, ask whether the role creates value nobody has created or protects value that exists. Experience helps protection (CFO, general counsel) and often hurts creation.
- **Parts:** The sort: "a way to sort these two things is if anybody in the world has done it before" (S027); for creation, hire learning slope, not expertise (S035); add only one or two doses of experience and ask them why, constantly (S061); domain experts are experts in the prior model (S029, S044); a big-company executive fits only if they still have something to prove (S028).
- **When to use:** A founder about to hire a famous industry veteran, or a first CFO, GC or VP.
- **How to score:** High if the experience hired matches a preservation role; low if a creation role is filled with someone whose edge is knowing what cannot be done.
- **Quote:** "if you're trying to create something and Forge something from scratch experience is often a handicap" (S067)
- **Sources:** 9 (S007, S009, S027, S028, S029, S035, S044, S061, S067)

## 15. One thing per person, ranked
- **What it is:** At PayPal each person, executives included, got exactly one problem and could not talk about anything else until it was solved. Most people solve B+ problems they understand instead of A+ problems they put off. He now narrows people to one to three things (S059).
- **Parts:** Rank priorities strictly and decide on the top one; never write pros and cons, which give every factor equal weight (S014, S028, S059); beware the kanban board where every card looks the same size (S014); fund the two to five ideas that can break out, not the 10 percent gains (S060); concentrate the best people (S016).
- **When to use:** A founder running several initiatives, a long roadmap, or a decision framed as a list.
- **How to score:** High if each key person owns one ranked problem; low if the best people are split across many.
- **Quote:** "most people will solve problems that they understand how to solve. Roughly speaking, they will solve B+ problems instead of A+ problems." (S006)
- **Sources:** 10 (S004, S006, S014, S016, S028, S048, S059, S060, S067, S068)

## 16. Promote the best practitioner to run the craft
- **What it is:** No general managers. The person who runs design is the best designer; the VP of product is the best product person and learns to manage later.
- **Parts:** It grooms undiscovered talent, lets the leader solve problems with their own hands, and people hate working for someone who cannot do the job (S014); mentor the best ICs into managers (S070); organize teams by craft (S087); promote only when the gap to the next best person is clear (S035).
- **When to use:** Filling a head-of role, choosing between an internal star IC and an outside manager.
- **How to score:** High if the leader is the best practitioner on the team; low if the plan is a professional manager over a craft they have not practiced.
- **Quote:** "believed in promoting the single best person designer engineer to run whatever the craft is" (S014)
- **Sources:** 8 (S006, S014, S025, S031, S035, S056, S070, S087)

## 17. Employee slope versus company slope (changed view on the ratio)
- **What it is:** Track each person's learning curve against the company's growth rate. Promote internally as far as the person's curve outruns the company's; bring in a relief pitcher when it does not, a little earlier than feels comfortable.
- **Parts:** LinkedIn grew slowly and Square fast, so the same person can fit one and not the other (S006); edit the team for this year's one to three challenges, not last year's (S028); managers usually leave the starter in too long (S067).
- **Ratio over time:** 2019: about 50/50 internal is the best case (S070). 2022: 70 percent internal is possible in a really healthy company (S061). 2024, marked current: about 70 internal to 30 external, under 50/50 is irresponsible and 100 percent internal fails (S049); a founder-mode company may hire only 30 percent externally (S035).
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to promote or hire over someone, or why a team stalled as the company grew.
- **How to score:** High if the person's slope is steeper than the company's; low if they are flat while the company doubles.
- **Quote:** "So always track like the individual slope of an employee and the company's growth rate." (S006)
- **Sources:** 8 (S006, S007, S028, S035, S049, S061, S067, S070)

## 18. Editor, not writer
- **What it is:** From Jack Dorsey at Square. The leader's job is editing: simplify with the red pen, ask clarifying questions, decide which book to fund, keep one voice, and edit the team.
- **Parts:** Use less red ink each month (S006); repeated red lines in the same part of the company mean a problem there (S049); ask on every action whether you are writing or editing (S056); "you want one voice one strategy and one uh Benchmark of quality" (S035); the CEO edits the team over time (S009, S028).
- **When to use:** A founder doing a function's work themselves, or judging whether a function head is working.
- **How to score:** High if the founder mostly edits and the red ink is shrinking; low if they are rewriting the same function's work every week.
- **Quote:** "you want to be an editor ideally as an executive not writing" (S018)
- **Sources:** 7 (S006, S009, S018, S028, S035, S049, S056)

## 19. Conviction by consequence, and task-relevant maturity
- **What it is:** A 2x2 for when to delegate: your conviction against how asymmetric the result is. Paired with Andy Grove's task-relevant maturity: the more times someone has done this exact task, the more rope.
- **Parts:** High conviction and high stakes, you decide every time; low and low, delegate every time; the middle boxes are judgment calls (S018, S035). Inspect daily on a first attempt, rarely once proven (S035, S049). The micromanage test: does this person know what they don't know (S056). Let a keen colleague run a low-stakes idea you doubt, so they learn to filter (S006).
- **When to use:** A founder unsure whether to step in, or a new executive asking how much rope they have.
- **How to score:** Low conviction plus low consequence that the founder still handles personally is a sign of poor leverage; high consequence handed to someone new to the task is a sign of abdication.
- **Quote:** "when you're in box one high conviction High asymmetry you have to make that decision every single time" (S035)
- **Sources:** 8 (S004, S006, S014, S018, S026, S035, S049, S056)

## 20. Startup as a movie
- **What it is:** You create product market fit, you do not discover it. Vision, script, cast, finance, direct, cut a trailer, sell tickets. If tickets do not sell, recut the trailer (message, channel) before you recut the movie.
- **Parts:** Ship full fidelity, not a light version (S011); find root causes fast and ship fixes (S057); release money in steps as each stage is proven (S027); change the movie only on an anomalous signal (S064).
- **When to use:** A launch that is not selling, a pivot debate, or a plan built on customer interviews.
- **How to score:** High if the team diagnosed whether the trailer or the movie is the problem; low if they are rebuilding the product without testing the message.
- **Quote:** "so it's not something you discover something you create" (S027)
- **Sources:** 7 (S003, S004, S011, S027, S028, S057, S064)

## 21. Speed: board-to-board fixes and a shrinking container *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Velocity of execution is the best predictor he knows of a company's success. Work expands to the time you give it, so keep shrinking the box.
- **Parts:** By the next board meeting, have found the root cause of what is broken and shipped a fix (S019); count days since founding on the first slide (S007, S084); decide within a day at about 70 percent confidence (S037); in the AI era add speed of adaptability, and drop the year-long roadmap (S007, S023, S051, S057).
- **When to use:** A plan with quarter-long milestones, a slow hiring process, or a debate that has run for weeks.
- **How to score:** High if fixes land between board meetings; low if the same issue appears on two decks in a row.
- **Quote:** "gas expands the size of the container so if you give people a quarter it'll take a quarter" (S035)
- **Sources:** 8 (S007, S019, S023, S035, S037, S051, S057, S084)

## 22. The board member as consigliere
- **What it is:** A board member has influence, not power. He asks questions, plays the founder's story back as a cartoonish mirror, and almost never prescribes. The one real board decision is whether to replace the CEO.
- **Parts:** Ask what you would want to know if it were your company (S060); state your level of conviction and the logic behind your gut so the founder can accept or reject it (S013); agree the realistic destination with the founder (S052); pick board members carefully, because they are hard to remove (S065); once there is a CEO, the CEO decides (S074).
- **When to use:** Board composition, a board fight, a founder asking how to use investors.
- **How to score:** High if the board brings frameworks and questions and the CEO owns the call; low if a board member is running the company or the founder took money from the wrong people.
- **Quote:** "it's just question question question it's never prescribing like an answer" (S053)
- **Sources:** 7 (S013, S023, S053, S054, S060, S065, S074)

## 23. Duct tape to metal; process only where it must be zero defect
- **What it is:** Early on, heroic effort holds the engine together. Each week, quarter and year, replace some duct tape with metal. Write process only for the things that must be zero defect or have asymmetric downside, because process repels the most talented people.
- **Parts:** Square split code into money-moving code (pair programmed, zero defect) and everything else (shipped alone, fast) (S057); processes freeze a startup (S067); heroic effort can last four to seven years (S055); process versus talent density (S058).
- **When to use:** A founder adding approval layers, a new ops hire who wants process, or a post-incident review.
- **How to score:** High if process exists only where failure is catastrophic; low if routine work needs sign-off.
- **Quote:** "constantly be turning duct tape and heroic effort into metal" (S035)
- **Sources:** 6 (S035, S037, S055, S057, S058, S067)

## 24. Divide by headcount, pair the metrics *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Two guards against gaming. Grade a manager on results divided by team size, which cures empire building. Pair every metric with its opposite, and put conflicting metrics under one owner.
- **Parts:** Fraud rate pairs with false positive rate; recruiter volume pairs with hire quality (S006); put growth and risk under one executive so the CEO is not making that trade every week (S035); an employee is an ingredient, and the dish cannot cost more than the customer pays (S085); be disciplined about leverage per employee (S051).
- **When to use:** Setting team goals, a manager asking for headcount, comp at AI-era salaries.
- **How to score:** High if every metric has a paired check and headcount requests come with output per person; low if teams are graded on volume alone.
- **Quote:** "divide by the number of people on their team and tell them that this is going to be their grade in their performance review." (S006)
- **Sources:** 5 (S006, S011, S035, S051, S085)

## 25. Judging and hiring executives *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Judge an executive by whether they work ahead of the business, and hire one in a function you do not know by learning from the best first.
- **Parts:** Ahead, just in time or behind: a function doing today what it should have done six months ago is a problem (S049, S056); the scorecard in S001 (secondhand) checks the first 90 days, peer feedback, the team they build, and whether they are right more often than not; for an unfamiliar function, meet the five best people in it (S056, S061), put an expert on the panel who can grade what you cannot (S033), and per S002 (secondhand) consider hiring a director first.
- **When to use:** Reviewing a struggling VP, hiring a first CFO, VP of Sales or Head of Product.
- **How to score:** The bar from his own words: "like six months to a year ahead of where you think you're supposed to be" (S056). An executive delivering just in time scores middle; one behind scores low.
- **Quote:** "not every lever can be tuned in a week most can't not every lever can be even tuned in a month" (S049)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001 secondhand, S002 secondhand, S033, S049, S056, S061)
