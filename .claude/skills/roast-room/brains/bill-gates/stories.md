# Stories: Bill Gates

Built from the ten extract-notes batches over 73 sources (1987 to 2026). 16 stories and cases he reuses to teach, most-told first, with the lesson he draws. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **The Altair and the founding.** He and Paul Allen had talked about the chip for years; when the Altair appeared on the Popular Electronics cover in Harvard Square, Gates thought "oh damn this is happening without us" (S048). They called MITS before the software existed, "Which was a good thing because we hadn't written the software yet." (S060), built BASIC on a PDP-10 simulator without seeing the machine, and it ran the first time Allen loaded it in Albuquerque (S001). He says dropping out felt low risk because he could go back; the scary part was hiring friends who moved their families (S048, S061, S038). Lesson: when the wave you predicted shows up, move at once, sell first, and build in the window you are given (S001, S017, S048, S053, S060, S062, S071).

2. **The IBM PC and the DOS deal.** IBM's Boca Raton team, told to ship in two years, used outside vendors and forecast 200,000 units; it sold over a million (S017). Microsoft pushed IBM from 8-bit to 16-bit (S005, S022), bought an operating system after Digital Research would not sign an NDA (S001), and put more people on the project than IBM did (S001, S022). IBM thought the hardware was the value: "they didn't see the values being in the software they thought that the hardware was the key" (S036). Microsoft kept the right to license to others. Lesson: keep the rights, expect clones, and let the partner who undervalues software make you the standard (S001, S005, S017, S022, S034, S036).

3. **OS/2 and the battle of the weaklings.** With IBM he backed OS/2 while Windows grew bottom up; he later called OS/2 "probably what is one of the most famous failures of all time in software we teamed up together with IBM to create the os2 operating system" (S005). IBM's 1,400 developers and its demand for 286 support made a compromised product, while neither OS/2 nor Windows sold until Windows took off in 1990 and DOS profits paid for the wait (S001). Lesson: a split strategy feels safe and is not; collapse to one (S001, S005).

4. **Windows and the developer flywheel.** The GUI bet took seven years from the 1983 announcement (S008) and was ridiculed until 1995 (S066). He built it the way he built DOS, by needing thousands of applications and pushing makers to stay compatible (S006): "the more windows we sold the more people developed applications and the more applications that were developed the more windows we sold" (S005). OEMs that bundled Windows gained share (S006). Lesson: the platform is made by the applications, and patience pays only if the base keeps growing (S005, S006, S008, S020, S066).

5. **The night at Xerox PARC.** In 1979 Charles Simonyi showed him the Alto; they spent hours making a typeset document and wrote down everything cheap computing should allow, and he hired Simonyi within two months (S025, S044). "Eventually, 15 years after Charles had showed me his thinking and we brainstormed, we largely achieved through Windows and Office on both Windows and Mac, we largely achieved that piece of paper." (S044) Xerox, with the most talent in one place, captured none of the value (S048). Lesson: a demo can set a company's agenda for 15 years, and the inventor is not always the winner (S025, S044, S048, S073, S076).

6. **The Mac partnership.** Microsoft put a team on Mac applications as large as Apple's and bet that graphical interfaces would follow the Mac (S036, S038, S066). "strangely we made more money when a Macintosh was sold than we did" (S017) on a PC, and he told Apple to license its software (S017). Lesson: write for a rival's platform if it grows your applications business, and both the licensed and the integrated model can win (S017, S036, S038, S066).

7. **Lotus and the software factory.** Single-product rivals could not move when GUIs and suites arrived; a Lotus founder told a panel "Bill's wrong but he works so much harder than us he's going to win" (S025). "Lotus had a great spreadsheet, but we moved faster than they did because we had this software factory mentality." (S053) Lesson: build the machine that makes software, not one product (S025, S053).

8. **Eight of the first 13 customers did not pay.** Early customers went bankrupt or paid partly, with Radio Shack and Apple among the few good ones (S017), so he kept a year of payroll in the bank (S062), later telling it as two years (S039). Lesson: hold a huge vision and a short survival horizon at once (S017, S039, S061, S062).

9. **Think Weeks.** He took "two full weeks, think weeks, one every six months, where I would just go off by myself" (S038) to ask whether Microsoft was on track and what the trends were, reading papers on paper and ending with strategy memos (S025). Staff worried about what he would decide (S016). The memos were what he reached for when the internet arrived and when software reliability was low (S038). Lesson: protect blocks of deep reading and turn them into a written call (S016, S025, S038, S063, S073).

10. **The Internet Tidal Wave, 1995.** The internet sat "as a fifth or sixth priority" (S062). After hours of browsing he found "almost no Microsoft file formats" (S078) and wrote: "Now I assign the Internet the highest level of importance." (S078) He forced a couple of months of email and retreats (S062), teams moved within months (S020), and a 12-month plan was laid out on 7 December 1995 (S020). Lesson: use the new thing yourself, admit the miss, and put the shift in every product plan (S017, S020, S025, S062, S078).

11. **Trustworthy Computing, 2002.** He listed the earlier top-priority memos (the internet, then .NET) and made trust the new one: "Trustworthy Computing is the highest priority for all the work we are doing." (S003) Lesson: restate the top priority when the platform's weak point changes, because people will not adopt what they cannot trust (S003, S038).

12. **Antitrust.** He saw the case first as a distraction he would win on the facts (S022, S009). Later: the remedies proposed "would have eviscerated Microsoft altogether" (S024), and his first legal team reinforced his narrow view until Brad Smith's team showed him he was wrong (S039). He regretted boasting about having no Washington office (S018, S037). Lesson: winning brings government, and relationships matter as much as being right on the law (S018, S024, S037, S038, S039).

13. **The mobile miss.** During the antitrust trial he wrote a memo saying never to assume phones would not get touchscreens, then made that mistake (S039). "we didn't assign the best people to do the work" (S037), the Motorola design win went to Android (S018), and "Microsoft would be far more valuable if we had one the mobile operating system competition Android is a huge asset for Google" (S018). Lesson: a shift inside your own skill set is the one you can least afford to staff weakly (S018, S037, S039, S050).

14. **Stepping back from Microsoft.** In 1998 he said he would pick another CEO in about a decade (S062); in 2005 he wanted a young leader to take product strategy (S063); in July 2008 he left full time, his first time since 17 without a full-time Microsoft job, with Ray Ozzie and Craig Mundie taking his responsibilities (S014). His fear was that the company would fade (S024); he says Nadella did better than he expected (S034). Lesson: plan the handover years ahead and stay close enough to review, not to run (S014, S024, S034, S037, S062, S063).

15. **The foundation as a bet portfolio.** A newspaper article on rotavirus killing half a million children a year showed him that no market signal pointed there (S068, S060, S065). He funds where markets do not, "the failure rate of many of these projects will be very high" (S068), and judges by measurable outcomes: vaccine coverage rose from about 70 to 86 percent of children (S061). He applied the same many-shots logic to energy, calling about 22 investors for Breakthrough Energy Ventures (S057) and expecting "eight teslas ten teslas" among many failures (S059). Lesson: a funding gap marks a bet worth making; place many shots and measure (S017, S057, S059, S060, S061, S065, S068).

16. **The AI turning point.** When founding Microsoft he wrote that "I may miss a bunch of breakthroughs in AI and that'll be what I give up to create this company" (S029). In mid-2022 he set OpenAI an AP Biology bar: "I thought the challenge would keep them busy for two or three years. They finished it in just a few months." (S076) At a September 2022 dinner at his house the model scored a 5 (S042, S044, S026), which he ranked "right up there with seeing the Xerox PARC graphics user interface that set the agenda for Microsoft for about fifteen years." (S042) In 2026 the threshold that changed his tone was coding: "the coding is the thing that I was obsessed with" (S033). Lesson: set a concrete test, and update fast when it is passed (S026, S028, S029, S033, S042, S043, S044, S076).
