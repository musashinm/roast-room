# Positions: Bill Gates

Built from the ten extract-notes batches over 73 sources (1987 to 2026). 39 topics, platform and technology-bet questions first, then the common founder questions. Dates follow `sources.md` (recording dates where known, upload dates for most videos). Where he changed his mind, both views sit side by side with dates and the newer one is marked.

## Platforms and developers
- **Stance:** A platform is made by the applications on it. Get millions of compatible machines out, make developers' work pay, and the count of applications becomes the moat.
- "The key to the competition in operating systems was getting lots of applications." (S001, 1993-11)
- "the more windows we sold the more people developed applications and the more applications that were developed the more windows we sold" (S005, 1992-07)
- "by having a platform out there that is on millions and millions of machines we can enable lots of applications to be written that could never have been justified in the past" (S005, 1992-07)
- "spend tens of millions developing software and sell it for $100 or so you've really got to get that base out there" (S068, 2008-02)
- He measures platform health in install base and app counts: 60,000 Windows applications in 1998, "60,000 applications is a uh uh a world's record" (S009, 1998-09); 1.5 million compatible devices for Vista (S013, 2007-01); 100 million Vista and 400 million Windows Live users (S014, 2008-01).
- **2023 to 2026:** the same frame applied to AI. "Agents will be the next platform." (S077, 2023-11) The personal agent becomes "your search UI your e-commerce UI your productivity UI" (S039, 2023-04), which makes the OS layer matter less.
- Sources: S001, S005, S006, S009, S013, S014, S068, S039, S077 (inferred from S001, S005, S068 for the stance).

## Licensing and keeping the rights
- **Stance:** Never sell the core outright and never give one customer exclusivity. License to everyone so the standard can spread past the first buyer.
- With IBM: "you didn't pay us enough for for to keep us happy the rest of our lives you don't own our company so we have to be able to sell it to other people" (S017, 2010-11)
- "os2 is non-exclusively licensed to ibm and so we are licensing it to the other manufacturers" (S015, 1987)
- To Apple on the Mac: "you please license your software it's the only way you can get the scale of Economics" (S017, 2010-11)
- MITS tried to lock up the BASIC licence and lost in arbitration; his lesson is to get the contract right (S001, 1993-11).
- He kept a no-modification clause for OEMs so every app had one base: "we believe that the benefit of having it be a solid base common for all applications justifies that restriction" (S005, 1992-07)

## Open versus closed
- **Stance:** "Open" to him means hardware choice and competition on one software standard, not shared source. Forked platforms (Unix) lose; integrated hardware and software (Apple) is a different model that can also work.
- "customers respond to having choice choice and competition" (S005, 1992-07)
- "a lot of that has to do with this openness the competition where hundreds of manufacturers are trying to provide the fastest the cheapest the smallest type of machine" (S022, 1991-07)
- On Unix: "you can't get commercial cooperation where you're out in the marketplace the next day trying to compete on those things" (S019, 2004)
- **2010:** concedes the closed model can win: "so you can do it on your own but it you know it's just a different model" (S017, 2010-11)
- **On AI models (2026):** "you can be open in the sense that you're free, you can be open in the sense that you're totally customizable," (S027, 2026-10), but powerful free models must sit "in a place where they are monitored" (S033, 2026-08).

## Standards and file formats
- **Stance:** Set the standard early, because once a format is established it almost never gets displaced. Then build on top of open standards rather than fight them.
- "we'd have a chance to set a binary standard so that the same system software and all the same programs would run on one machine" (S001, 1993-11)
- "Once a format gets established it is extremely difficult for another format to come along and even become equally popular." (S078, 1995-05)
- "The strength of the Office and Windows businesses today gives us a chance to superset the Web." (S078, 1995-05)
- "it's very hard to take a leading software product and replace it you know it doesn't happen it shouldn't happen very often" (S034, 2025-02)
- **Gap he names for AI (2023):** "There isn't yet a standard protocol that will allow agents to talk to each other." (S077, 2023-11)
- Sources: S001, S005, S009, S034, S047, S071, S077, S078.

## Network effects
- **Stance:** Share begets share where people exchange documents or content, so a good-enough product with high share pulls further ahead.
- "there's a tendency for people with very high share to get higher shares" (S016, 1995-04)
- "the more users it gets, the more content it gets, and the more content it gets, the more users it gets" (S078, 1995-05)
- "the more people are connected the more people want to be connected" (S017, 2010-11)
- His learning-loop version: "you're in the learning Loop if you have a very popular software product your customers are telling you how to improve it" (S025, 2025-02)

## Partners and complementors
- **Stance:** No single company builds a platform alone. Pick complementors who lead their own layer, push them to move first, and stay untied.
- "No single company is going to be able to do this." (S007, 1990-11)
- On Intel: "they're not tied to us we're not tied to them but because they're the leader in in their field and we're the leader in ours we've been able to work together and grow the industry" (S020, 1996-11)
- "the strength the windows has always come from the ecosystem around it" (S013, 2007-01)
- He staffs partner projects harder than the partner: more people on the IBM PC than IBM (S022, 1991-07), and on Mac apps "we actually put more people on the project than Apple did" (S036, 2016).
- Content he partners for rather than builds: "we won't become a studio. We'll have to work with studios." (S016, 1995-04)
- Warning from 1987: "everyone relies on their best customer quite a bit" (S015, 1987), so do not depend on one.

## Pricing
- **Stance:** High volume at a low price. Software has huge fixed cost and no marginal cost, so price to reach the whole world, then earn it back on scale.
- "this high volume low cost model, you know, works very well" (S071, 2004-02)
- "we have a scale economic business with huge fixed R&D costs and no marginal cost" (S025, 2025-02)
- "there are a lot of desks. I don't have to make that much money per desk." (S024, 2025)
- To get a standard out, skip big fees: "because we didn't insist on significant payments, because we wanted it to get out there" (S001, 1993-11)
- Per-seat test against the cost of a worker: "pay us less than $100 a year for the right of that person to have the very best software" (S071, 2004-02)
- Because software never wears out, you only get paid for real advances: "we have to innovate enough that it's worthwhile to license" (S071, 2004-02)

## Free, discounting, and piracy
- **Stance:** Give away the client piece when it makes the paid platform more valuable. Treat piracy as a pricing and adoption problem, not an outrage.
- "By giving away the client code to support all of these scenarios, we can start to show that a Windows machine on the Internet is more valuable" (S078, 1995-05)
- **1998:** in China, "as long as they're going to steal it we want them to steal ours" (S062, 1998-05)
- **2009, on his 1976 hobbyist letter:** "my first and last you know kind of outraged" (S070, 2009); "being upset about it is never effective" (S070)
- Against free competitors: "they have to make it better than what it's ever free and be able to charge for that" (S070, 2009)
- No recorded position on discounts as a sales tactic.

## Business model: recurring, ads, subscriptions
- **Stance:** Treat a customer as a stream. On the web he leaned to ads over subscriptions in 1996; for AI agents in 2023 he flips to paid, so the agent works for the user.
- "since the notion of recurring revenues is central to the the key businesses that we're in" (S023, 1996)
- **1996:** "as soon as an electronic community charges a subscription, the number of people who visit the site drops dramatically" (S002, 1996-01)
- **2023 (newer):** "I think you'll pay for most of them, which means companies will have an incentive to make agents work on your behalf and not an advertiser's." (S077, 2023-11)
- On bolting AI onto old products and charging more as the shallow first stage: "everybody adding AI to every application and saying, okay, pay me extra cuz now I got a little AI." (S051, 2024-10)

## Content
- **1996-01:** "Content is where I expect much of the real money will be made on the Internet, just as it was in broadcasting." (S002)
- **1996 (later that year):** "I can guarantee there will be no content club in my lifetime" (S023) near Disney's revenue, and media would never be bigger than applications: "not reasonable to contemplate those activities being larger than our applications group in this decade" (S023)
- **Read:** bullish on content as a market for others, cautious about Microsoft owning much of it; it stayed a small, experimental bucket (inferred from S002, S020, S023).

## Betting on where technology goes
- **Stance:** Assume compute, storage, and bandwidth become nearly free, then ask what that allows in 10 years. The applications are hard to predict; the price points are not.
- "because the chips were improving exponentially, doubling every couple of years, it allowed us to think of the computing part as essentially being free." (S038, 2025-02)
- "I don't know what the Breakthrough applications are but I know when you get to the right price points" (S049, 2011-10)
- "There is no slowing as we look at the next decade in terms of the processor speed or memory size" (S008, 1995-11)
- He forecasts in 10-year blocks: "this whole idea of information at your fingertips will have advanced dramatically in that 10-year period" (S022, 1991-07); "that's where things take 10 years to get done" (S066, 2007-05)
- His one stated miss in this frame: Microsoft assumed hardware would get infinitely good but not that phones would get touchscreens and big processors (S039, 2023-04).
- Sources: S008, S022, S035, S038, S039, S048, S049, S053, S066, S068.

## Timing and patience with a bet
- **Stance:** He is usually early, and he knows it. Stay with a real shift for years until the enabling pieces arrive, but admit when you overreached.
- "if you find an idea that requires three or four years of improvement and patience and really sticking with it that we're very good at that" (S020, 1996-11)
- GUI announced in 1983 took seven years; the 1986 CD-ROM push took until 1995 (S008, 1995-11).
- IPTV: "we got in probably five years before we needed to it's just now starting to happen" (S063, 2005-09)
- "sometimes I'm overoptimistic on these things" (S073, 2019-07)
- Tablets, a bet he would not drop: "all the portable devices will become uh tablet devices" (S071, 2004-02); "I'm un unrepentant on uh my belief in" (S066, 2007-05)

## The internet
- **Before 1995:** online services and CD-ROM were his favored network; the internet sat low on the list. "the internet came along and we had it sort of as a fifth or sixth priority" (S062, 1998-05, looking back). In April 1995 he still hated the "information superhighway" term and called dial-up the wave of the future without making it the priority (S016, 1995-04).
- **1995-05 (newer):** "The Internet is the most important single development to come along since the IBM PC was introduced in 1981." (S078) "Now I assign the Internet the highest level of importance." (S078)
- **1996:** "this is the most important thing it's our biggest opportunity it's our biggest challenge" (S020, 1996-11)
- **2010 and 2025, looking back:** "we kept expecting it to happen and it never happened it never happened" (S017, 2010-11); "it was true we had to really adjust to stay on top of it" (S025, 2025-02)

## Mobile
- **2005 to 2008:** bullish on Windows Mobile through carriers and makers: "we've got more than a 100 smartphones out now uh with 93 mobile operators in 55 countries" (S012, 2006-01); phones are rich enough to run a broad range of applications (S014, 2008-01).
- **2019 to 2025 (newer):** his biggest miss. "we missed being the dominant mobile operating system by a very tiny amount" (S037, 2019) "we didn't assign the best people to do the work" (S037) "something that was clearly within our skill set we were clearly the company that that should have achieved that and we didn't" (S018, 2019-06) "I certainly, you know, messed that up in, in a huge way." (S050, 2025-03)
- He blames distraction from the antitrust trial and weak staffing, and says the software momentum went to Android after a Motorola design win (S018).

## Search and cloud
- **2005:** "we're playing a tiny bit of ketchup on that" (S063, 2005-09) on search; in 2008 he expected search to stop being ten links and wanted more than one serious player (S048, 2008-03).
- **2019:** names the cloud miss for enterprise vendors: "the natural companies to do the cloud would have been your classic enterprise vendors IBM Oracle sa P" (S018) who "should feel bad that they didn't they didn't get ahead of Amazon" (S018).
- **2023:** missing the first search engine was outside Microsoft's scope at the time, and Google was born on that wave (S039).

## AI: from long-term dream to "the age of AI"
- **1993 to 2013:** a distant goal. "the computer will eventually learn to reason in somewhat the same way that humans do" (S001, 1993-11); "general artificial intelligence, this is the holy grail" (S071, 2004-02); "AI has turned out to be a tough problem" (S048, 2008-03); "very hard to predict at least five times as long as Ray Kurzweil says" (S069, 2013-05).
- **2019:** "I would start an AI company that whose goal would be to teach computers how to read" (S018, 2019-06)
- **2023 (turn):** "The development of AI is as fundamental as the creation of the microprocessor, the personal computer, the Internet, and the mobile phone." (S076, 2023-03) "here's a case where we underpredicted that natural language and the computer's ability to deal with that" (S044, 2023-03)
- **2024:** "AI is is not pets.com. This is something very, very fundamental." (S067, 2024-07)
- **2026 (newest):** a different class of technology. "anyone who analogizes AI as a technology to other technologies is missing that this time is different." (S033, 2026-08) "This makes nuclear weapons look like nothing." (S026, 2026-09) He still says "No, I'm not a doomer." (S027, 2026-10)
- What moved him in 2026 was coding: "starting late last year, as the models, particularly Claude at first, but now uh many of the models were very good at coding" (S033).

## AI agents and the software industry
- **Stance:** Agents replace apps, search, and shopping sites, and no one company will own them. Software gets built differently, but someone still decides what is worth building.
- "They'll replace search sites because they'll be better at finding information and summarizing it for you." (S077, 2023-11)
- "I don't think any single company will dominate the agents business--there will be many different AI engines available." (S077, 2023-11)
- "the whole complexion of the software market, how many applications will there be?" (S051, 2024-10)
- Access will spread like chip design did after fabs split off: "access to these kinds of models will be democratized uh and you know it'll be whoever comes up with the best ideas around them." (S039, 2023-04)
- **2025:** fewer founders can get in front now: "but it'll be, you know, two or three out of ten thousand" (S050, 2025-03); "I do think that hyper competition will be beneficial to users" (S034, 2025-02)
- **2026:** "I fundamentally think people will have personal agents." (S027)

## AI and jobs
- **2019:** a good problem: "the increased productivity that will come from AI will create dilemmas about what should people do with that extra time and you've got to consider that a good thing" (S018, 2019-06)
- **2023:** "The hardest question to answer is the job market." (S042, 2023-08)
- **2024:** "No one expected the white-collar thing to come before the blue-collar thing." (S051, 2024-10)
- **2026 (newest, sharper):** "there's no doubt to date AI has created more jobs than it's destroyed." (S026) but "I am extremely concerned about uh the labor market effects, uh you know, within the next 2 years in the white-collar space" (S033, 2026-08). His fix is tax: shift from taxing labor to taxing capital (S025, S033, S041).

## AI safety and regulation
- **2023:** "Governments need to work with the private sector on ways to limit the risks." (S076, 2023-03) "It's not the end of the world if if we our technology adoption is somewhat slower than the optimal." (S039, 2023-04)
- **2026 (newer, firmer):** "you can't rely on the industry to self-regulate here." (S026) "It's not enough to have a kill switch." (S028) Voluntary review "doesn't do anything" in his words: "That's doesn't do anything." (S028) Yet no pause: "And you know, I won't be saying we should have an overall pause or or slow down." (S028)
- The near-term threat he ranks first: "the thing that's urgent has to do with bad people using AI, not the AI going off on its own." (S028, 2026-09)

## Long-term R&D
- **Stance:** Fund research with a long horizon once the core business makes money, keep it open to academia, and pull it toward product over time. Most businesses underinvest.
- "the best investments we've ever made as a company is having our Microsoft Research Group" (S072, 2005-07)
- "we thought, wow, we've got money left over." (S073, 2019-07)
- On a $6.8 billion budget: "it's kind of an intimidating number, at least to me uh since uh 10 years from now people will say to me whether that was wise or or not" (S071, 2004-02)
- "it's surprising to me how low the levels of real research investment uh by businesses are" (S048, 2008-03)
- Sources: S011, S014, S048, S066, S071, S072, S073.

## Competition and paranoia
- **Stance:** Assume you are one step from losing the lead. Watch the shift, not the rival, and out-commit single-product competitors.
- "we're one step away from not you know leading here we got to got to keep doing better" (S036, 2016)
- "I was always Running Scared" (S034, 2025-02)
- "the high technology business is not one where you you get to take your future for granted" (S020, 1996-11)
- "we didn't think as much about about our competitors as they thought about us" (S025, 2025-02)
- "Lotus had a great spreadsheet, but we moved faster than they did because we had this software factory mentality." (S053, 2025-02)
- Early on he was blunt: "And we made darn sure they never came near to what we had done." (S001, 1993-11)

## Antitrust and government
- **1991 to 1998:** a distraction, and the facts would win. "it's not something we expect to to change our business but it is a distraction" (S022, 1991-07); "There is no lack of innovation. There is no lack of investment. There is no lack of competition." (S009, 1998-09)
- **2019 to 2025 (newer):** regret. "eventually I came to regret that statement because it was kind of almost like taunting Washington DC" (S037, 2019); "there was way more than just understanding law that went into handling government relationships." (S039, 2023-04); "that I didn't have to talk to politicians. Well, that was a mistake." (S038, 2025-02)
- Cost: "there were literally proposed remedies that would have eviscerated Microsoft altogether." (S024, 2025)
- Prediction he accepts: "there will be more regulation of the tech sector" (S037, S018, 2019)

## Raising money and going public
- **Stance:** Capital was never the constraint in software. He took outside money for the board and went public for employee liquidity.
- "our business is not one where money makes that much difference at all" (S062, 1998-05)
- "we took a million dollars for 5% of the company" (S061, 2017-01); he never spent it (S061). He says the VCs were taken for the board they brought (S039, 2023-04).
- On the IPO: "We didn't use any of the money that we raised." (S001, 1993-11) "It was really just to get the incentive system for the employees" (S071, 2004-02)
- **For AI-scale bets (2024):** "the capital costs were reasonably significant, almost at the edge of what venture would ever be comfortable with." (S043, 2024-01)

## Debt and cash reserves
- **Stance:** No debt recorded; instead, hold enough cash to survive with no revenue. The bar rose over the retellings.
- **1998:** "i wanted to have enough money in the bank to pay a year's worth of payroll" (S062)
- **2017:** "I always made sure we had enough money in the bank to pay everybody for at least a year if nobody paid us at all." (S061)
- **2023:** "I wanted to have enough in the bank if no customer paid us to pay the payroll for two years." (S039)
- Origin: of the first 13 customers, eight did not pay in full (S017, 2010-11). "I was very risk-averse in in running the company." (S061, 2017-01)

## Hiring smart people
- **Stance:** Hire raw ability, young, and train them in your way; then add experienced managers as you scale. He later corrected the IQ-only view.
- "we wanted them to come with clear minds, not polluted by some other approach" (S001, 1993-11)
- "we are hiring IQ like mad" (S034, 2025-02)
- "we were an engineering company we were about how you hire smart people and how you use tools to develop software broadly we were global and we weren't about a single product" (S018, 2019-06)
- **2005:** the rare hire bridges engineers and customers: "the greatest missing skill is somebody who's both good understanding the engineering and has good relationships with the hardcore engineers and bridges that to working with the customers and the marketing and things like that" (S072)
- **2025 (newer):** "I'd only have super high IQ people you know and and you know so everybody would know how to program" (S035), told as a belief that was wrong; "Steve helps me hire some even more experienced people, and we become a bit more professional" (S053).
- Talent over assets: "all the products are going to be obsolete very very rapidly" (S062, 1998-05)

## Co-founders and partners in the company
- **Stance:** His best decisions were people. A technical founder needs a partner who brings the people side.
- "we would not have achieved anything what we did without those partnerships" (S061, 2017-01) on Paul Allen and Steve Ballmer.
- "I'm bringing kind of a technical view, he's bringing a people view, and that works phenomenally." (S024, 2025)
- He credits Allen for the vision and Ballmer for creating the company (S039, 2023-04, paraphrase).

## Firing
- No recorded position.

## Equity and options
- **Stance:** Share equity widely with strong contributors early, and judge yourself by delivery, not the stock price.
- "Microsoft had started giving out stock options to people as early as 1981." (S001, 1993-11)
- "We don't want to get ahead of ourselves uh in terms of that that type of measure because it's a secondary measure" (S016, 1995-04)
- "the whole notion of what was the company worth or what the stock was worth wasn't that interesting" (S017, 2010-11)

## Focus versus diversification
- **Stance:** Stay in software, horizontal rather than one product, and enter only where engineering talent decides the winner. In a new category, be first or second or leave.
- "what we're all about is one thing, developing software" (S071, 2004-02)
- "As long as it was software where development talent was the key to doing it well." (S001, 1993-11)
- "For each category we are in we will have to decide if we can be #1 or #2 in that category or get out." (S078, 1995-05)
- After OS/2: "what we've decided to pursue is a single strategy not a strategy split into low" (S005, 1992-07)
- Personal focus: "I had to suppress my sort of normal desire to be polymathic and be monomaniacal." (S051, 2024-10)

## Side projects
- No recorded position.

## Niche entry for a hard platform
- **Stance:** When the mass-market price is out of reach, bootstrap volume in a high-value niche and ride the learning curve down.
- "it's nice that we're able to bootstrap the volume with tasks, like inspecting an airplane engine, that's very high-value" (S073, 2019-07)
- On PARC's Star: people would not pay for word processing at that price, so you had to enter at the low end (S044, 2023-03, paraphrase).

## Marketing, ads, and brand
- **Stance:** He sold the idea of software as much as the product, and treats reputation as a platform asset that one bad product can damage.
- "I was going out and telling people about the magic of software" (S036, 2016)
- "our reputation as a company has in turn become more vulnerable" (S003, 2002-01)
- "If we don't do this, people simply won't be willing -- or able -- to take advantage of all the other great work we do." (S003, 2002-01)
- No recorded position on paid ads for a founder's own product.

## Culture
- **Stance:** Culture is the founder's job: frugal, intense, engineering first, with blunt product reviews.
- "the atmosphere of this company is one of its key assets and it's part of my job to create it" (S015, 1987)
- On flying coach, "a weenies culture, not a shrimp culture." (S016, 1995-04, as noted)
- "I still do product reviews where I'll say hey this feature is stupid" (S035, 2025-02)
- "I've made more of my mistakes in that area maybe than than any anywhere." (S066, 2007-05), on getting broad-skilled people to work together.

## Work hours and burnout
- **1987:** "the long hours i work no they're not for everybody i don't don't try and impose them on everybody" (S015)
- **1995:** "lot more time getting people to work less than we do to work more" (S016)
- **Looking back (2016 to 2025):** "I didn't believe in weekends until I was about 30 I didn't believe in in vacations at all" (S036); "for my 20s I was monomaniacal I did not read broadly" (S025); in his 20s he was weak at managing others (S050, paraphrase). By 53 he was more mellow (S039).
- No recorded position on burnout as such.

## Remote work
- No recorded position on remote versus in-office work. Nearest: he wanted developers in private offices, "developers should just have the quiet of their own office" (S072, 2005-07), and in 2020 said "the internet is helping reduce the pain a little bit" (S046).

## Metrics
- **Stance:** Volume and installed base prove a platform; the market is a clean scorecard; log problems as data and fix the top causes.
- "We were doubling every two years." (S001, 1993-11)
- "the marketplace would tell you when you got it right when you got it wrong" (S017, 2010-11)
- "we've observed over a billion sessions of people new using this new version of office" (S013, 2007-01)
- Support calls logged by cause with a numeric goal (S006, 1991-05).
- **2021, the warning:** "our metrics had us focus on the easy stuff" (S059)

## Succession and stepping back
- **1998:** "i will pick somebody else to be ceo" (S062)
- **2005:** "i need to get one of the great young people at microsoft to sort of get on the hot seat of setting the product strategy" (S063)
- **2008:** goes full time to the foundation, with Ray Ozzie and Craig Mundie taking his responsibilities (S014, 2008-01).
- **2025:** "the one thing that plagued me was, was I going to see the company fade in terms of its excellence" (S024); "I always thought oh God I'm going to leave and the company's going to get screwed up and I'm not going to know what to do" (S034)

## Selling the company
- No recorded position. He never sold Microsoft and talks only about the IPO and succession (see above).

## Portfolio bets and failure rates
- **Stance:** For hard problems with unknown answers, fund many shots, expect most to fail, and judge by the few that work. He applies this to energy, vaccines, and AI.
- "thousands of things go on in parallel" (S064, 2009-11)
- "that's what capitalism is good at at picking lots of wild ideas and uh continuing to back the ones that that work" (S048, 2008-03)
- "one of them is going to give us much cheaper power without causing any problem we don't know which one it is" (S064, 2009-11)
- "even though we'll have a high failure rate i think there's enough there that we have a likelihood of of substantial success" (S059, 2021-10)
- A funding gap is the signal: "because the poorest people don't create a market that there's not much research into things like a malaria vaccine" (S017, 2010-11)
- He admits the bias: "you know, I'm a little over focused on the scientific solution." (S050, 2025-03)
