# Frameworks: Bill Gates

25 frameworks, roughly most-used first, built from the extract notes on 73 sources (1987 to 2026: his own memos and oral history, keynotes, testimony, and long interviews). "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated thresholds and habits, not quotes. Keynotes (S005 to S014) show how he framed things on stage, so rules lean on his own text and oral history (S001 to S003, S076 to S078) where possible.

---

## 1. The platform flywheel: more machines, more applications *(unnamed; he calls it a "virtuous cycle")*
- **What it is:** A platform wins when its installed base draws developers, and their applications sell more copies of the platform. He says DOS used it to pass five rival systems and Windows repeated it. The same loop runs for users and content on the Web.
- **Parts:** Get the base out (many compatible machines, low price); win developers with volume, tools and every hook they ask for; count applications as the scoreboard; let falling hardware prices grow the base again.
- **When to use:** Any question about building a platform, a marketplace, an API, a developer program, or an ecosystem play.
- **How to score:** 5 if each new user makes the product more valuable to builders and each new builder brings users, and there is a plan for the first turn of the loop; 3 if the loop exists but depends on one partner or one hit app; 1 if it is a product with an "ecosystem" slide and no reason for third parties to build. He uses install-base counts and application counts as proof, not press (inferred from S006, S012, S014).
- **Quote:** "the more windows we sold the more people developed applications and the more applications that were developed the more windows we sold" (S005)
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S005, S006, S007, S013, S014, S017, S020, S022, S068, S071, S078)

## 2. Scarce to free *(unnamed; "free computing" is his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Find an input improving exponentially, project when it goes from scarce to effectively free, and ask what people will do with it then. He and Paul Allen made this bet on chips in 1974 and 1975 and saw software as the only limit. Since 2023 he applies it to intelligence.
- **Parts:** Name the exponential (chip cost, storage, bandwidth, model cost); assume the resource will be nearly free; design for the cheap, powerful machine that is coming, not today's; reset your own cost priors, because experienced people stay anchored on scarcity (S049).
- **When to use:** A founder is pricing, designing, or sizing a market around today's cost of compute, storage, bandwidth, or AI inference.
- **How to score:** High if the plan gets better as the input gets cheaper; low if it depends on the input staying expensive or scarce. Ask what happens to the business when the cost falls 10x.
- **Quote:** "it goes from being super scarce to super free" (S025)
- **Changed view:** In the PC era the free thing was computing (S001, 1993; S053, 2025). In 2025 he says intelligence is now going free and calls that "even more profound than Computing becoming free" (S034). Newer view. His one admitted miss on this bet: Microsoft assumed hardware would get infinitely good but not that phones would get nice touchscreens and big processors (S039, 2023).
- **Sources:** 12 (S001, S008, S024, S025, S034, S035, S038, S039, S048, S049, S051, S053)

## 3. Don't miss the turn in the road *(unnamed; "what are we missing")*
- **What it is:** Even the leader misses turns, and the turn you miss is the one that ends you. Every day ask what is moving in adjacent fields, what research or feedback you are ignoring, and whether the right people are hired for it. He cites IBM, Digital Equipment and Wang as great companies that lost their way.
- **Parts:** Scan adjacent fields; read customer feedback and research; ask who would beat you if they realized what you realized; staff the new thing with your best people; repeat daily.
- **When to use:** The founder is winning and planning to coast, or is dismissing a new technology because current customers do not ask for it.
- **How to score:** High if there is a named watch list of threats and someone senior owns each; low if success is the main argument. Missing a shift that turns out huge is worse than being early (S063).
- **Quote:** "let's try to make sure today's not the day we miss the turn in the road" (S062)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S018, S020, S024, S025, S034, S036, S039, S045, S062, S063)

## 4. Year versus decade *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** People overestimate what changes in a year or two and underestimate what changes in ten to fifteen. Natural interfaces and new devices argue for years, then cross a "magic threshold" (S048) and the debate ends. He admits his own dates run early.
- **Parts:** Separate "is it coming" from "when"; expect a slow, embarrassing first phase (GUI announced 1983, took about 7 years; tablets "like Windows 1992"); keep funding through it; watch for the threshold of price, size, and usability where ordinary users switch.
- **When to use:** Timing a bet on a new technology; deciding whether a slow start means the idea is wrong.
- **How to score:** Ask the founder to state the 10-year end state and the 2-year reality separately. Good: confident on direction, humble on date, funded for a long first phase. Bad: a plan that needs the shift in 12 months.
- **Quote:** "people tend to overestimate what can change in a year or two and they underestimate the cumulative effective change that can take place" (S048)
- **His own record:** "in the past I've often been overly optimistic about how quickly these things would roll out" (S016). He was early on pen computing, speech (off "by couple decades or so", S068), tablets, interactive TV, and the internet arriving ("we kept expecting it to happen and it never happened", S017). With large language models he underpredicted (S044, 2023). IT successes make people over-optimistic about the pace in energy (S054).
- **Sources:** 11 (S008, S016, S017, S019, S044, S048, S049, S054, S066, S068, S073)

## 5. License to everyone and price low for volume *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Own the software layer, license it to every hardware maker, never give one customer exclusivity, and price low so the standard spreads. Software has huge fixed cost and no marginal cost, so volume decides everything.
- **Parts:** Keep the right to sell to others (the IBM terms); price for design wins and momentum, not margin per unit; let makers compete on speed, price and portability; collect a small amount on a very large base.
- **When to use:** Pricing a platform, an API, a protocol, or anything whose value rises with the number of users.
- **How to score:** High if price is set to win the standard and the contract keeps distribution open; low if one partner gets exclusivity or price is set for margin before the standard exists. His own test from the Forbes list: with that many desks you do not need much per desk (S024).
- **Quote:** "for software and price your software super low so that you're winning these design wins, and accumulating momentum." (S053)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S005, S015, S017, S022, S024, S036, S045, S053, S071)

## 6. Green premium and the learning curve
- **What it is:** His metric for climate bets, and the clearest view of how he measures a hard-tech bet: the extra cost of the clean version of a product over today's. A product is adopted at scale, especially by middle-income countries, only when that premium is near zero. Costs fall only by building real projects that ride a learning curve, as solar, wind and lithium-ion did.
- **Parts:** Size the problem against one ruler (51 billion tons a year, or "50 billion and zero", S051); price the premium by sector; pick paths that can come down the curve; fund supply (R&D, venture) and demand (early buyers, the Catalyst fund) together; a venture screen of at least half a percent of emissions per company (S037, S058).
- **When to use:** A deep-tech or hardware bet that needs a cost curve to work; any "the market will pay more because it is better for the world" pitch.
- **How to score:** High if the founder knows today's cost gap in numbers and the volume at which it closes; low if the plan relies on buyers paying a premium forever or on subsidies that can be repealed.
- **Quote:** "the gold standard metric is the scale of the green premium" (S057)
- **Sources:** 10 (S018, S037, S039, S046, S051, S055, S056, S057, S059, S067)

## 7. Partner for variety, or build the whole product *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Choose by market. Where innovation across many makers is a net gain (PCs, phones, robots), supply the platform and let partners build devices and content. Own the whole product only where tight integration has to come first (Xbox, Surface). He calls the PC and Apple models different, and says both can work.
- **Parts:** Ask whether variety or integration wins this market at this stage; supply the shared layer (rights management, runtime, services); count partners and compatible devices as the measure; let partners do what you would not.
- **When to use:** Build versus partner decisions; whether to make hardware; whether to open a platform to third parties.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can say why this market rewards variety or integration right now; low if the choice is habit. Early-stage markets often need both done together (S066).
- **Quote:** "We think it's beneficial to us that even if we did a few ourselves, it wouldn't give us what we have through those partnerships." (S066)
- **Sources:** 9 (S007, S011, S012, S013, S016, S017, S020, S064, S066)

## 8. Technology bets as warrants *(unnamed; his analogy)*
- **What it is:** Picking which technologies to bet on is like holding warrants: be contrary, leave some things aside, go where it is not hot, and hold many positions because most will fail and a few will pay for all of them. In climate he wants "200 crazy people" per path and expects most to be wrong.
- **Parts:** Many independent shots per problem; accept a high failure rate; fund on the size of the win if it works; reserve capacity before you know the winner (the 700 million dollar antibody check, S074); keep going after failures (Aquion, A123, Kior, S059).
- **When to use:** Portfolio decisions; a founder choosing between one big bet and several smaller ones; R&D allocation.
- **How to score:** Guidance: a hard problem with one shot scores low no matter how good the shot looks. High if there are several approaches, each with a clear kill test.
- **Quote:** "those are a bit like warrants where you really have to be willing to be contrary to leave some things aside" (S063)
- **Sources:** 9 (S046, S048, S054, S055, S058, S059, S063, S064, S074)

## 9. Long-term research before you need it *(unnamed; includes his "two boundaries")*
- **What it is:** Fund research not tied to the shipping cycle, so that when a shift arrives you can respond (he credits this for Bing and AI). Research pays off only if two boundaries are managed: academia versus commercial work, and research groups versus product groups. Keep the lab open to outside approaches.
- **Parts:** A standing research budget even when managers see no return; hire researchers who will say another group is doing it better (S073); TechFest-style handoffs to product teams; watch the outside world for faster progress.
- **When to use:** A founder deciding whether to spend on anything beyond the next release; a profitable company with no research.
- **How to score:** High if some share of effort is on the 5 to 10 year problem and there is a path from research to product; low if all effort serves the next quarter. He says he is surprised how little real research businesses fund (S048).
- **Quote:** "I'm a huge believer that long-term Investments that aren't necessarily connected to your current shipping cycle" (S034)
- **Sources:** 9 (S011, S014, S034, S043, S047, S048, S071, S072, S073)

## 10. The milestone demo test *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Before believing a new capability, set a hard, concrete bar it cannot fake, and change your mind when it clears it. For GPT he set an AP Biology exam on questions outside the training set. He ranks two demos as agenda-setting: the Xerox PARC graphical interface (1979 or 1980) and GPT in 2022. After the PARC demo he and Charles Simonyi wrote down what cheap computing would allow, and Windows and Office reached it about 15 years later (S044).
- **Parts:** Write the bar in advance; make it non-memorizable; when it is cleared, ask at once what it means for every product area; write the 5 to 10 year list of what is now possible.
- **When to use:** Judging hype; deciding whether a demo is a trick or a platform shift.
- **How to score:** High if the founder's claim rests on a hard test passed; low if it rests on a curated demo or on the critics being wrong. Do not dismiss a capability for flaws that are not fundamental: "Every example they give of a specific thing it doesn't do, wait a few months." (S044)
- **Quote:** "I’m not going to pay any attention until you can get a 5." (S042)
- **Sources:** 9 (S025, S026, S028, S034, S039, S042, S043, S044, S076)

## 11. Bot versus agent, and agents as the next platform
- **What it is:** A bot lives in one app, acts when asked and forgets. An agent works across apps, suggests on its own, remembers and improves. He says agents will replace search, shopping and productivity interfaces, upend the software industry, and make the operating system layer matter less. He has written agent memos since the 1990s.
- **Parts:** Works at the level of tasks, not files; cross-app memory; user keeps the final decision and controls data access; business model paid by the user, not an advertiser; no standard protocol for agents yet (as of 2023).
- **When to use:** Any AI product; any software business whose interface or distribution an agent could take over.
- **How to score:** High if the product is where tasks get done or the data an agent needs; low if it is a feature an agent will absorb. He sees adding AI to every app and charging extra as a shallow first stage (paraphrase of S051).
- **Quote:** "Clippy has as much in common with agents as a rotary phone has with a mobile device." (S077)
- **Sources:** 8 (S027, S034, S039, S051, S061, S073, S076, S077)

## 12. The top-priority memo and the deliberate crisis *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** When a shift he ranked low proves deeper and faster than planned, he creates a crisis on purpose: a couple of months of email and retreats, then a few ideas, then internal measures and a public plan with a date. He restates the company's single top priority every few years (the Internet in 1995, .NET, Trustworthy Computing in 2002, simplicity in 1998).
- **Parts:** Rank the shift as "highest level of importance"; put it in every product plan; assign owners with numbered lists; announce a crisp plan (the 7 December 1995 Internet day); revise as the market moves.
- **When to use:** A founder who sees the shift but whose team treats it as one priority among many.
- **How to score:** High if one priority is named and changes what every team builds; low if the shift is a side project or a separate division. He rejects a separate "Internet" partition (S023).
- **Quote:** "Every few years I have sent out a memo talking about the highest priority for Microsoft." (S003)
- **Sources:** 7 (S003, S009, S016, S020, S038, S062, S078)

## 13. Backcast from the pervasive end state *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Describe what the world looks like once the technology is everywhere, about ten years out, then work back to the building blocks needed. He rejects framing an era as who is doing a deal with whom. His check on a good scenario: critics call it too conservative and too futuristic in equal measure (S008).
- **Parts:** A 10-year scenario that needs no new inventions; the building blocks (networks, cheap devices, tools for non-experts, applications from other industries); design around scenarios across devices, not one device.
- **When to use:** Setting product strategy; checking whether a roadmap aims at today's market or the one coming.
- **How to score:** High if the founder can describe the end state concretely and name what must be true by when; low if the plan only extends this year's product.
- **Quote:** "I think the best approach is to really look at what will it be like once it's pervasive." (S008)
- **Sources:** 7 (S007, S008, S011, S014, S016, S044, S071)

## 14. Paradigm bet to enter a market, and stay a generation ahead *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Do not fight the entrenched leader on the current generation. Bet on the next paradigm and enter there. Microsoft let Lotus and WordPerfect own character-mode DOS and went after the Mac and Windows with Word and Excel. New companies win with an insight incumbents lack, often because incumbents see computers as rare and expensive.
- **Parts:** Name the next paradigm; build for it natively, not as a port; accept a long wait for the hardware to catch up; find the flaw in the incumbents' thinking.
- **When to use:** A startup facing a strong incumbent; choosing whether to target the current or next platform.
- **How to score:** High if the plan rides a shift the incumbent is structurally slow to adopt; low if it is a better version of the incumbent on the incumbent's ground (see framework 23: there is only a 1-2-3 market).
- **Quote:** "So the question was what was the next paradigm shift that would allow for an entry." (S066)
- **Sources:** 7 (S001, S005, S020, S025, S053, S066, S071)

## 15. A year of payroll in the bank *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** After early customers failed to pay (eight of the first 13 did not pay in full, S017), he held enough cash to pay everyone for a year with no revenue. No long-term debt, low leverage, no development money from customers.
- **Parts:** Cash floor of one year of payroll; no leverage; take outside money for advice, not need.
- **When to use:** Raising, spending, or hiring decisions that thin the cash cushion.
- **How to score:** Guidance: under a year of runway with no revenue is a risk he would flag; leverage is a red flag.
- **Quote:** "I always made sure we had enough money in the bank to pay everybody for at least a year if nobody paid us at all." (S061)
- **Inconsistency:** In S039 (2023) he says two years: "I wanted to have enough in the bank if no customer paid us to pay the payroll for two years." (S039). Most sources, including the newest (S045, 2025), say one year.
- **Sources:** 7 (S015, S017, S039, S045, S061, S062, S064)

## 16. Safeguards before slowdown *(unnamed; his AI risk frame, 2026)*
- **What it is:** The near-term AI risk is bad people using the most powerful tools (cyber, bio), not the AI going off on its own. The answer is required monitoring and records, not a pause or a kill switch. Once there are many frontier competitors, responsibility moves from companies to government. A job falls to AI only when the system is trusted to be more reliable than people; coding crossed first.
- **Parts:** Required safeguards and monitoring; open weights allowed only on platforms that keep the monitoring; licensing tied to monitoring; judge risk by capability, not by AGI or consciousness; check what share of the economy has elastic demand before assuming jobs come back.
- **When to use:** An AI company's safety, open-source, or go-to-market decisions; any founder betting that regulation will stay light.
- **How to score:** High if the plan assumes required safeguards and survives them; low if it depends on voluntary review or liability law, which he calls "kind of absurd" as a safety system (S027).
- **Quote:** "putting in the safeguards and the monitoring will not meaningfully slow things down." (S026)
- **Changed view:** In 2023 he called AI risks real but manageable (S076). From 2026 he says he is raising an alarm and that this time is different (S033, S026, S027, S028). Newer view.
- **Sources:** 7 (S026, S027, S028, S033, S038, S039, S043)

## 17. The bootstrap check *(unnamed; "bootstrap" is his word)*
- **What it is:** A platform needs volume, volume needs applications, applications need volume. Before betting, check that a new wave has a clear bootstrap: who pays for the first turn. Interactive TV failed because box and network cost far exceeded what movies on demand brought in. Hard platforms can bootstrap with a high-value niche (HoloLens on airplane engine inspection, tablets with doctors and claim writers).
- **Parts:** Name the first paying use; check that its value covers the build cost; find a vertical niche that tolerates today's price; wait for critical mass before asking content makers to invest.
- **When to use:** Two-sided products, new devices, infrastructure plays.
- **How to score:** High if a narrow first use pays its own way now; low if the business only works after the network is big.
- **Quote:** "things to get bootstrapped you need volume but to get volume you need applications but to get applications you need volume" (S020)
- **Sources:** 6 (S005, S012, S020, S039, S048, S073)

## 18. The software factory *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Do not build one product; build the machine that produces software faster than anyone (best hiring, best tools, testing), and sell it worldwide. Single-product rivals (Lotus, WordPerfect, Ashton-Tate, MicroPro) could not move when the graphical interface and suites arrived.
- **Parts:** Hire for raw ability; invest in internal tools; a full product line sharing code; global sales from early on; cost control.
- **When to use:** A one-product company deciding whether to build a second product or deepen the first; build-the-team versus build-the-product questions.
- **How to score:** High if the company gets faster at shipping with each product; low if each product is a one-off.
- **Quote:** "the key thing is that we decided we would be a software factory. We would hire the best software people, we'd write the best software tools." (S053)
- **Sources:** 6 (S001, S018, S023, S025, S034, S053)

## 19. Embrace, superset, and carry the installed base forward *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** When a new open standard appears, adopt it and add your own value on top rather than fight it. Answer a rival's clean-slate design by evolving the installed platform so users keep their hardware and applications. Do not attack an installed base head on.
- **Parts:** Adopt the standard (HTTP, HTML, XML); build it into the platform; stay backward compatible; ship pieces together to lower migration hesitation.
- **When to use:** An incumbent facing a new standard; a startup thinking of displacing an installed base.
- **How to score:** For an incumbent: high if the new standard becomes part of the core product. For a challenger: low if the plan asks users to throw away what they own.
- **Quote:** "The strength of the Office and Windows businesses today gives us a chance to superset the Web." (S078)
- **Related line:** "the installed base is the defender of itself" (S022)
- **Sources:** 6 (S009, S013, S020, S022, S023, S078)

## 20. The PC model: specialize, and standardize where innovation is over *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The winning industry structure is many firms each owning a piece (chips, software, applications) with standards so the pieces fit. Their pooled R&D beats any vertical rival. Standardize the parts where there is no innovation left; compete where it is unclear which approach is best. Unix fragmented because vendors who competed could not cooperate.
- **Parts:** Pick your layer; push standards for the commodity layers; keep competing on the open layers.
- **When to use:** Deciding which layer to own; whether to back an industry standard.
- **How to score:** High if the company owns a layer whose value grows as others commoditize around it; low if it owns a commodity layer.
- **Quote:** "the industry's done very well at uh latching on to standards for the things that there were no longer any innovation in" (S066)
- **Sources:** 6 (S005, S009, S010, S019, S022, S066)

## 21. The most demanding customer, and feedback as data *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Pick the customer recognized as the most demanding in an area and manage them in their business. It cuts marketing studies and puts you ahead of customers who do not yet ask. Treat support calls, betas and crash reports as data: log them, find the top causes, set a numeric target. Ask customers what code they should not have to write.
- **Parts:** One demanding lead customer; support-call database with top causes; large betas (400,000 for Windows 95, S016); a numeric goal (halve calls per copy, S006).
- **When to use:** Choosing a launch customer; deciding what to fix next.
- **How to score:** High if feedback is counted and drives the roadmap; low if it is anecdotes. He says it failed in enterprise systems where Microsoft had no natural feel (S039).
- **Quote:** "if you pick the most demanding customer in an area who is viewed as recognized as the most demanding customer and then you just manage them in their business. It simplifies things a lot." (S039)
- **Sources:** 6 (S006, S009, S020, S022, S039, S048)

## 22. Think week *(his name)*
- **What it is:** Two weeks a year (one every six months as CEO) offline with papers and books, ending in a strategy memo on whether the company is on track and what the trends are. Paired with a yearly audit of his calendar to cut what was not needed.
- **Parts:** Time blocked and uninterrupted; reading sent in by staff; a written memo at the end; a time audit.
- **When to use:** A founder too deep in operations to see the shift; a strategy question that needs reading, not meetings.
- **How to score:** High if the founder has protected time to think and writes the conclusion down; low if strategy is done in the gaps.
- **Quote:** "i take two weeks a year to just go off and read and think where i'm not interrupted by work or anything else" (S063)
- **Sources:** 6 (S016, S025, S038, S062, S063, S073)

## 23. The learning loop and lock-in of a popular product *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A popular product gets customer feedback, user habits, file formats and add-ons that do not port, so rivals must clone it exactly. In products that exchange documents, high share gains share. He told Lotus's rivals there is no spreadsheet market, only a 1-2-3 market.
- **Parts:** Own the format; own the habit; extensibility that only works on you; competitors must beat you at your own product.
- **When to use:** Attacking or defending a category leader; deciding whether file formats and integrations matter.
- **How to score:** For a challenger: low if the plan is a "better category" product against an entrenched habit. For an incumbent: high if format and extensions keep users.
- **Quote:** "you're in the learning Loop if you have a very popular software product your customers are telling you how to improve it" (S025)
- **Sources:** 5 (S010, S016, S025, S034, S078)

## 24. Build it into the platform *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Put hard, shared services (security, transactions, directory, install, natural input, cloud storage) into the platform once so no developer has to. Spend most where the platform is most used, because each improvement multiplies through everyone who builds on it.
- **Parts:** Ask builders which systems code they keep writing; fold it into the platform; build in what only works if every part uses it.
- **When to use:** Platform and API roadmaps; deciding what is platform and what is application.
- **How to score:** High if the platform removes work every developer repeats; low if it makes developers buy a runtime or solve the hard parts themselves.
- **Quote:** "this is something the software industry will build into the platform so individual developers don't have to go off and do that complicated work" (S014)
- **Sources:** 5 (S009, S010, S012, S013, S014)

## 25. Within our ambit *(his phrase)*
- **What it is:** Enter only where your engineering culture is the key to winning. Software where development talent decides the outcome was in scope; games and payroll packages were not (S001). Mobile operating systems were in scope and Microsoft lost them anyway, which he calls his biggest mistake; social networks and the first search engine he says were outside it.
- **Parts:** Ask whether your core skill decides this market; say no to the rest (services, hardware, chips, most content, S023); if it is in scope, assign the best people.
- **When to use:** Diversification, new product lines, side bets.
- **How to score:** High if the new market rewards the skill that won the first one; low if it needs a different culture. If it is in scope and you staff it weakly, he says that is how you lose it (S018).
- **Quote:** "As long as it was software where development talent was the key to doing it well." (S001)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S018, S023, S039, S048)
