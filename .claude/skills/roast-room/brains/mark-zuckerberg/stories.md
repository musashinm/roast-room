# Stories: Mark Zuckerberg

20 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws (and, where the record shows it, the lesson he skips). Built from the twelve batches of notes on 81 sources; S020 and S021 are one recording, cited as S020. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **"It clearly wasn't going to be us."** The night Facebook launched at Harvard he went for pizza with friends and said someone would build this for the world, and it would not be them; Microsoft and Google had the engineers. Lesson: incumbents had the resources but did not care, and "we just kind of cared more than those other companies about making it exist" (S052). Told in S013, S047, S052, S053, S054, S064, S065, S066.
2. **College Facebook and the first lockdown (2005).** An exact clone with the same name launched at schools Facebook had not reached, and the team "literally did not leave the house until we had addressed the problem" (S053). Clones that lodged abroad, like VKontakte in Russia, held him off for nearly a decade, and he sued StudiVZ in Germany while saying he would win by being better. Lesson: answer a copycat at once and go international early (S053, S057). He admits he does not know whether ignoring College Facebook would have changed anything (S053).
3. **Turning down Yahoo's $1 billion (2006).** With about 10 million users, management wanted to sell and the board tried to fire him. He said no without a spreadsheet, and "the whole management team was gone within um within about a year after that" (S054). Lesson he draws: explain the long-term vision early, and set up governance so you cannot be fired for wanting to build (S054, S012, S018, S020). He also says he got lucky that News Feed and open sign-up proved him right within months (S054).
4. **The vesting mistake.** Peter Thiel required founder vesting, Zuckerberg had never heard the term, and the early equity split with Eduardo Saverin came apart. Lesson: "that mistake probably cost me billions of dollars but it's fine" (S053). Move on from expensive errors.
5. **Small teams with one social feature beat whole startups.** From 2004 to 2007 two or three people built photos, groups and events in a couple of months. Photos had no high resolution, printing or album reordering, and still became the most used photo product, because sharing reached all your friends at once. Lesson: the social feature "was more important than every other feature put together" (S055). Told in S055, S058.
6. **Facebook Platform overload (2007).** Within a week of launch apps had millions of users, and people added 50 to 100 app boxes to their profiles. The feed rule rewarded adding boxes, so he changed it to give reach in proportion to engagement, accepted that some developers lost growth, and launched Connect slowly in a closed beta. Lesson: design incentives on purpose, and make painful platform changes rarely (S056, S069).
7. **Buying Instagram (2012).** In emails to his CFO he wrote that Instagram "can hurt us meaningfully without becoming a huge business" and that "what we're really buying is time" (S011). It had about 16 employees and 20 million users when it joined (S013). Lesson he draws later: Meta's growth playbook made it bigger than it would have been alone (S059, S016). The emails also show the deal as neutralizing a rival, which he walked back 45 minutes later (S011).
8. **WhatsApp for $19 billion (2014).** About 70 percent of users opened it daily, and price talks started only once the two sides agreed on the vision (S074). The first move after the deal was to drop the one dollar fee (S059). Lesson: engagement and shared vision justify the price; he allowed "I could be wrong" (S074).
9. **The HTML5 bet and the mobile rewrite (2012).** Facebook built its mobile apps as a web shell for speed; it felt bad, so he paused all features for about a year and rewrote them native while mobile ads did not yet exist. Lesson: "when you're losing it's usually pretty clear what you have to do" and the test is pain tolerance (S020). He calls it a year and a half mistake.
10. **Retiring "move fast and break things" (2014).** The 2012 IPO letter made it a core value (S001). By 2014 fixing bugs took more time than the speed gained, so the motto became move fast with stable infrastructure, which "may not have quite the same ring to it" (S070). Lesson: every value costs something; name what you give up (S047, S009, S071). In 2026 he held the Muse agent back three months for privacy and security and called it the right call for the business too (S037).
11. **The News Feed redesign on big monitors (2013).** Designers built a spacious feed on large screens; most people saw one and a half stories instead of two or three and used it less. A year of work was rolled out to a few people and stopped. Lesson: the team had a blind spot from its own computers; test on a slice before you ship (S062).
12. **Forcing Messenger out of the app (2014).** He made people install a separate messaging app and took heavy criticism. Lesson: a short-term painful move was the only way to deliver quality messaging, but "maybe we didn't handle that as smoothly as we could have" (S062).
13. **Cambridge Analytica (2018).** He designed the platform, tightened it in 2014, and accepted a developer's legal certification that data was deleted. Lesson: audit, never take a certification at its word, and "if someone's going to get fired for this, it should be me" (S009). Told in S009, S072; by 2024 he argued the data was not usable and that taking blame early drew more attacks (S028).
14. **The 20-year mistake (2016 onward).** He treated political attacks after 2016 as a corporate crisis and took responsibility for everything, and became a target. Lesson he now draws: own what is yours, be firm about what is not, fund outside research in advance; "the political miscalculation was a 20-year mistake" (S020). Told in S020, S028, S014.
15. **Open Compute saved billions.** Google built distributed infrastructure first and kept it private. Meta, second, had no edge to protect, so it published its server and data center designs, the supply chain standardized on them, and costs fell by billions. Lesson: open what is not your advantage and let the ecosystem make it cheaper; he applied the same logic to Llama (S007, S018, S020, S041, S074).
16. **Late to Reels, then doubling the GPUs.** Meta still thought feed was where people interact when TikTok rose, so Reels came late and the clusters were too small. He ordered enough GPUs for Reels plus a second service of the same size, which looked wasteful and later carried the AI push. Lesson: "most of the times where we make some decision that ends up seeming good is because we messed something up before" (S018). Told in S018, S022, S028, S081.
17. **Metaverse first, then AI first (2021 to 2026).** He renamed the company, spent more than $10 billion a year on Reality Labs, and expected holograms before advanced AI (S005, S013, S020). By 2026 AI had come first and was bigger, and he cut the VR roadmap to breakthrough releases only (S079). Lesson he draws: the tech tree moved in an unexpected order, and the metaverse idea stands (S037). The unstated lesson: years of spend on the wrong sequence, held because he controls the vote (inferred from S013, S020, S079).
18. **Three direct reports and the year of efficiency (2022 to 2023).** He planned for a permanent pandemic boom, then revenue fell for the first time (S048, S080). He had his team count managers' direct reports, found about three on average, and flattened the company, cutting around 10,000 jobs (S016, S006). Lesson: every layer slows decisions, indirect costs compound, and leaner went faster than he expected (S006, S080).
19. **Quest Pro, Ray-Ban Meta and the Orion bar.** Quest Pro at $1,500 sold fewer than planned, because consumers are cost conscious (S027). The second Ray-Ban glasses sold about five times the first and still sold out (S028, S030). Orion missed his price and form-factor bar, so it became an internal dev kit (S028). Lesson: hardware often lands on the second or third version, and supply has to be bet before demand is known.
20. **Llama 4 and the lab reboot (2025).** Llama 4 beat Llama 3 but was not on a frontier trajectory. He admitted he had assumed Meta's strength in other machine learning would carry over (S031), cleared his calendar for a month or two to meet researchers, and rebuilt the lab around a small, flat, talent-dense team with far more compute per researcher (S023, S030, S036). Facing a check of tens of billions for compute before the models existed, he asked: "I'm like, Alex, are you sure this is going to work?" (S023). Lesson: reboot rather than patch, and size a giant bet by its backstop (S023, S038).
