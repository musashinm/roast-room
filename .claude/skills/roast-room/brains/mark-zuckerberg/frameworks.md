# Frameworks: Mark Zuckerberg

21 frameworks, roughly most-used first, built from the twelve note batches over 81 sources (his letters and essays, the 2012 internal emails, long interviews, town halls, keynotes and earnings calls, 2005 to 2026). "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea is used (ids listed; S020 and S021 are one recording and count once). Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The How-to-score lines are scoring guidance for the council built from his stated reasoning, not quotes. Where he changed his mind, the newer view is marked.

---

## 1. Open source as a competitive weapon *(unnamed; "open when you are second")*
- **What it is:** Open the infrastructure layer when it is not your edge, either because a rival built it first or because you do not sell it. The industry standardizes on your design, supply costs fall, outsiders improve it, researchers want to join, and no gatekeeper can cut you off. The product layer on top stays proprietary.
- **Parts:** (1) Do we sell this layer? He had no cloud business, so giving infrastructure away cost nothing. (2) Are we leading or second? Google built distributed infrastructure first, so for Meta it was no advantage and might as well be open. (3) Will an ecosystem make it better and cheaper (Open Compute, PyTorch, React, Llama)? (4) Could the open thing become the product itself? Then it is "a trickier economic calculation" (S018). (5) Is the capability now dangerous, or mainly helping competitors? Then hold it back.
- **When to use:** A founder deciding whether to open-source a tool, SDK, model or spec; a smaller player facing a better-funded closed leader; anyone whose costs depend on a supply chain that could standardize on their design.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the layer is not what the company sells, a closed leader already owns it, and an open version would pull in contributors and drive down the founder's own costs; 3 if open helps adoption but the founder has no plan for where the money comes from above it; 1 if the thing being opened is the product itself, or it hands the founder's only edge to a bigger rival.
- **Change over time:** In 2005 he kept Facebook's caching and search layers closed, partly to keep the competitive advantage (S050). By 2023 he called open sourcing models "net positive" (S016) and in 2024 wrote that Llama should become the standard (S007). Newer view (July 2025 on): selective. He writes about being "careful about what we choose to open source" (S008), says Meta will "continue to not open source everything" (S082), and in 2026 says the open models are not strong enough to rely on for Meta's own core stack (S083). He calls himself not a zealot on it (S020, S041, S031).
- **Quote:** "okay not an advantage for us because Google already has that so uh we might as well just make it open" (S020)
- **Sources:** 19 (S006, S007, S008, S016, S018, S019, S020, S027, S032, S035, S041, S042, S043, S045, S050, S074, S081, S082, S083)

## 2. A new computing platform every 10 to 15 years *(unnamed; with the "richest format" ladder)*
- **What it is:** Every 10 to 15 years a new computing platform arrives (mainframe, PC, phone, then glasses), each more natural and more ubiquitous than the last, and apps get rewritten for it. Media climbs the same ladder: text, photos, video, immersive scenes. Plan around that curve the way Intel planned around Moore's law, because platforms get defined early.
- **Parts:** Name the platform that comes next; place your product on the format ladder; expect a new major format every three to five years that adds to the last rather than replacing it (S028); expect the old device to stay but get used less (S033, S036); each platform brings its own input method (S036).
- **When to use:** Timing a technology wave; deciding whether a product is too early or too late; asking which format a new consumer product should be built in.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the product rides the next format or platform while the current one still pays the bills; 3 if it is a better version on the current platform with no path to the next; 1 if it depends on a platform shift arriving on a specific date the founder cannot control.
- **Where he got the timing wrong:** In 2015 he gave mainstream VR five to ten years (S064, S075) and said AR would reach phones before glasses (S065). By 2024 he said AI would arrive before AR holograms, the reverse of his earlier prediction (S020), and in 2026 he said the metaverse order was wrong and AI is "so much bigger" (S037, S079). He also wrote that people overestimate two years and underestimate ten (S085).
- **Quote:** "every 10 or 15 years a completely new Computing platform comes along" (S068)
- **Sources:** 18 (S005, S009, S013, S017, S025, S028, S032, S036, S037, S048, S054, S062, S066, S067, S068, S071, S075, S077)

## 3. Many AIs, not one *(unnamed; later "personal superintelligence")*
- **What it is:** Reject the picture of one central superintelligence run by one lab. Build many agents: a personal assistant for every person, an AI for every creator and every business, and spread the technology widely so power stays checked and balanced. He uses it to position Meta against closed labs and to justify open releases and free tiers.
- **Parts:** A base assistant; creator agents; business agents that sell only that business's products (S027, S043); distribution as safety, where wide access checks bad actors (S007, S031); superintelligence aimed at creation, not automation (S079); personal devices that see and hear what you do (S008).
- **When to use:** An AI startup choosing between one general product and many specialized ones; any decision where a few providers could end up controlling a founder's core capability.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder's product gets better as more agents and models exist, and does not depend on one provider's goodwill; 3 if it builds on one closed model but could switch; 1 if the whole business is a thin layer on a single closed model that could absorb it.
- **Quote:** "I don't think we necessarily want there to be like one big super intelligence." (S017)
- **Sources:** 18 (S007, S008, S014, S016, S017, S018, S023, S027, S031, S032, S033, S035, S038, S041, S043, S045, S079, S083)

## 4. The platform tax: own the next platform *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Estimate what living on someone else's platform costs you, in fees and, worse, in product features you are blocked from shipping. His rough figure for Meta is about twice the profit if it owned the platform. If the tax is large and you can afford it, build toward owning the next platform (glasses, mixed reality, your own models) rather than fighting for better terms on the current one.
- **Parts:** Count the money (app-store fees, ad-tracking rules); count the blocked features, which offend him more than the fees (S022); check whether the gatekeeper is also your competitor; decide whether you are big enough to build your own base layer; if not, diversify across platforms.
- **When to use:** A founder whose distribution runs through an app store, a marketplace, a cloud or a model provider that competes with them.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the dependency is a direct competitor that already blocks features, and the founder has a credible path to a channel it does not control; 3 if the dependency is a neutral supplier with fair terms; 1 if the founder plans to build a whole new platform to escape the tax with no cash engine to pay for it.
- **Limits:** This is a giant-company move. He says Meta can do it because "we're a big enough company at this point" (S020). In 2026 he reframes it: owning a platform is "less about wanting to own a platform for its own sake" and more about building the best things (S023). In 2015, asked about depending on Android, he said only that it was "a little bit stressful" and the answer was building what people want (S040).
- **Quote:** "I think we might be like twice as profitable if we own the platform or something" (S020)
- **Sources:** 16 (S005, S007, S014, S018, S020, S022, S023, S026, S028, S029, S030, S035, S040, S041, S048, S070)

## 5. Sell the device at cost, earn on usage *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Price the hardware or base service for reach, not margin: devices at cost or subsidized, a free consumer tier, and money from software, services, ads, results or a small take on transactions once the community exists.
- **Parts:** Devices at cost or subsidized (S005, S077); pick a price that does not cap the community (S027); a free ad-supported tier with a paid tier for heavy compute (S019); businesses pay only for results (S083); a very small cut when users make money (S031, S079).
- **When to use:** Hardware, platform or AI products where usage creates the value, and a founder is tempted to charge a premium up front.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder has a second revenue line that grows with usage and can fund the subsidy; 3 if usage revenue is plausible but unproven; 1 if the plan is to subsidize the device with no cash to cover the gap.
- **Where it went wrong:** Quest Pro at $1,500 sold fewer than planned, and he concluded consumers are "very cost conscious" (S027). The model needs a cash engine he has and most founders do not.
- **Quote:** "The strategy overall is not to make money on the hardware, but to make it so that it can help develop the ecosystem and then over time the business model will be based on software and services." (S029)
- **Sources:** 14 (S005, S016, S017, S019, S023, S027, S029, S030, S031, S032, S067, S077, S079, S083)

## 6. The learning organism *(his phrase: "companies are learning organisms")*
- **What it is:** Strategy is less any single move than how fast the company learns what its users want. Ship early, run many versions at once, read results against a baseline, and the one who learns fastest is ahead by version three or four.
- **Parts:** Any engineer can ship a version to 10,000 or 100,000 people; compare against the baseline; good results become the new baseline; bad ideas get written down so nobody repeats them (S065); do not ship something so bad that nobody uses it, or you collect no data (S020).
- **When to use:** Product roadmaps, competitive races where both sides have similar resources, and any founder who wants to debate an idea for weeks instead of testing it.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder can put a version in front of real users within days and read the result; 3 if testing takes a release cycle of months; 1 if the plan rests on one big launch with no way to learn before it.
- **Quote:** "we can learn faster than every other company we're going to win" (S020)
- **Sources:** 11 (S001, S009, S016, S020, S026, S047, S054, S062, S063, S065, S066)

## 7. Talent density: the smallest group that can hold it in their heads *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** For frontier or hard problems, do not spread people and money across many efforts. Put a small group of the very best people on it, give them a lot of compute or resources each, keep the org flat, and set no top-down deadlines on research.
- **Parts:** 50 to 100 people for a frontier lab, not hundreds (S036); every seat is precious; no non-technical management layers; the founder recruits personally; no team bigger than its stage deserves, or people lose the agency to change things (S020).
- **When to use:** Staffing a new bet, a research effort or a rebuild; a founder who wants to hire ahead of the work.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if a small team of proven people owns the whole problem with resources to match; 3 if the team is right-sized but thin on top talent; 1 if headcount is the plan.
- **Quote:** "you really want the smallest group that can hold the whole thing in their head" (S082)
- **Sources:** 11 (S020, S022, S023, S030, S031, S036, S045, S055, S058, S075, S082)

## 8. Open versus closed ecosystem in every generation *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Each computing generation produces a closed integrated stack and a more open, partnership-based one, and it is not settled in advance which wins. PCs went open (Windows); mobile went closed (Apple), and value flowed to the closed side. He treats the next generation as a reset and plays the open side, competing on price and developer freedom.
- **Parts:** Name the closed player; take the open side with side-loading, low creator fees, interoperable items and open standards (S005, S077); partner for what you cannot do (Ray-Ban, Microsoft, Adobe); compete on affordability.
- **When to use:** Positioning against an integrated incumbent; deciding whether to open APIs or let users leave easily.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder is the open alternative in a market whose closed leader taxes or blocks developers, and can be cheaper; 3 if open but with no cost or ecosystem edge; 1 if the founder is building a closed copy of the incumbent without its integration.
- **Limits:** He concedes "Apple pretty clearly won this generation" (S041), and his own store kept some fees higher for a time (S077).
- **Quote:** "in each generation of computing that I've seen so far, you know, PC's, mobile, there's basically an open ecosystem and there's a closed ecosystem." (S029)
- **Sources:** 10 (S005, S007, S020, S027, S029, S033, S035, S041, S048, S077)

## 9. Buying time *(his words, 2012 emails)*
- **What it is:** A nascent social product can hurt you before it is a real business, because networks have network effects and there are only so many social mechanics. Buying it early buys a year or more before anyone else reaches scale on that mechanic. Buy where you are vulnerable, and buy what you would otherwise only regret not buying.
- **Parts:** Is it a network with a meaningful brand, even with little revenue? Are we vulnerable there (mobile, in 2012)? Could I scale it to a billion people (S027)? Agree on the vision before the price (S074). Let it run on its own (S074). Often the point is the team, not the product (S049).
- **When to use:** A founder watching a smaller rival grow in an area where they are weak; any acquisition, partnership or acqui-hire decision.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the target owns a mechanic or audience the founder cannot copy and is growing in the founder's weak spot; 3 if it is mainly a talent buy; 1 if the price buys revenue the founder could build, or if the founder cannot afford to keep it alive.
- **Limits:** These are a giant's moves. The emails drew a congressional hearing; 45 minutes after writing them he sent a clarification that he did not mean buying them to stop them competing (S011). He later defended the Instagram deal on outcomes (S059). A small founder rarely has the cash; the startup version is partnering early or hiring the team.
- **Quote:** "what we're really buying is time" (S011)
- **Sources:** 10 (S011, S013, S016, S027, S049, S054, S059, S068, S074, S076)

## 10. Build ahead of what you cannot see, sized by a backstop *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Buy capacity ahead of the next thing, because being caught short is worse than overbuilding. He learned it when Reels left Meta short of GPUs against TikTok. He sizes the bet by its downside: what share of spend it is, what the worst case is worth, and what would have to be true for it to fail.
- **Parts:** Buy for the curve, not today's demand (S018); the three conditions for a giant bet are a business model that supports it, a CEO who believes, and the ability to build (S030); state the share of spend and the floor (VR at under 20 percent; Xbox as the downside case, S048); the backstop test, where the bet fails only if the whole field fails (S023); hardware supply is decided before demand is known (S028).
- **When to use:** Capacity, inventory, compute or hiring decisions ahead of demand; any founder asked to commit before the product exists.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the downside is survivable and the asset can be reused or resold; 3 if the bet is large but the core business covers it; 1 if being wrong ends the company.
- **Where it went wrong:** He read the 2021 pandemic as a permanent pull-forward and overplanned spend (S048). He said in 2022 Meta would "lose a lot of money for a long time" on the metaverse (S013), and in 2025 that misspending "a couple of hundred billion dollars" would be "very unfortunate" (S030). His asymmetry is explicitly "for a company like Meta" (S030).
- **Quote:** "We always want to have enough capacity to build something that we can't quite see on the horizon yet." (S018)
- **Sources:** 10 (S013, S018, S022, S023, S028, S030, S048, S081, S082, S083)

## 11. Spark, retention, growth, then monetization *(unnamed; "build, then monetize")*
- **What it is:** Four steps in order for any new product: make something people enjoy, make it retain, grow the community, and only then turn on revenue. Retention is the gate before asking anyone to sign up. He usually waits until hundreds of millions or a billion use something before focusing on the money.
- **Parts:** Spark; retention, measured as how often people come back (daily, weekly, monthly in 2005, S051); growth through the product itself, not marketing (S054); monetization last; keep scaling at a loss only while scale gives a strategic edge (S080).
- **When to use:** A founder deciding when to charge, when to spend on growth, or whether to scale a product that loses money per user.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if retention is proven and the growth comes from users bringing users; 3 if growth is real but bought; 1 if the founder wants to scale or monetize before people come back on their own.
- **Limits:** It assumes a cash engine paying the bills. He says Meta "can do the long-term stuff because we do really well at the core business" (S022). In 2005, without one, he kept operating costs low and stayed cash-flow positive (S051).
- **Quote:** "step two, retention, step three, growth and scaling the community. And then only at that point is step four, which is monetization." (S027)
- **Sources:** 9 (S027, S029, S054, S056, S057, S074, S080, S081, S082)

## 12. Learn from others, build the better version *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** No company invents everything. When someone else proves a format (Stories, short video, text discussion), learn from it, then build the better version and push it through your own distribution. Invent some things yourself, or you are not a real company.
- **Parts:** Has a rival proved the format works? Do we have distribution they lack? Can we make it better, not just the same? Are we late, and how late can we afford to be?
- **When to use:** A founder deciding whether to copy a rival's feature, or facing a bigger player who may copy theirs.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the format is proven and the founder brings real distribution or a real improvement; 3 if the copy is equal and the founder must win on execution; 1 if it is a straight copy with no distribution edge.
- **Change over time:** In 2012 he told founders that companies "just trying to copy" others "aren't aren't Successful" (S052), and in 2009 he said he would win against a German clone by being better (S057). Clones also beat Facebook for years in Russia (S053). Newer view (2024): learning from others is fine as long as you build the better version (S020). He admits Meta was late on Reels because it still thought of feed as the place to interact (S028, S022).
- **Quote:** "we're not embarrassed about learning from things that other people like discovered that we're good first and and then we build the better version of it" (S020)
- **Sources:** 9 (S016, S020, S022, S027, S028, S029, S052, S053, S057)

## 13. Would I work for them? *(his hiring rule)*
- **What it is:** Only hire someone to report to you if you would work for that person. Applied at each level, it cascades down the company.
- **Parts:** Raw intelligence first, alignment second (S051); not skills or years in the role (S050, S054); would I report to this person; later, would I want to be friends with them (S038).
- **When to use:** Any senior hire, especially a founder's first direct reports.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder would genuinely report to the candidate; 3 if strong but clearly below the founder's bar; 1 if the hire is being made because there is more work than people.
- **Quote:** "I've developed over time a simple rule which is that I will only hire someone to work directly for me if I would work for that person" (S075)
- **Sources:** 6 (S009, S015, S016, S038, S053, S075)

## 14. Values are what you give up *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A value is only real if a good company could do the opposite, and if you can say what you will lose for it. His main example: move fast cost bugs, and later it cost engineers moved onto infrastructure.
- **Parts:** State the value; name the cost; operationalize it in real processes, or it is words on a website (S026); revisit when the cost changes.
- **Change over time:** Move fast and break things was a 2012 value (S001). In April 2014 he retired it for move fast with stable infra, because fixing bugs was costing more time than speed saved (S070, S047). He later said the old line was used in ways he did not mean (S009).
- **When to use:** A founder writing values, or arguing for speed without saying what they will give up.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder names the price and has a process that pays it; 3 if the value is stated with no cost named; 1 if every value is something no one would disagree with.
- **Quote:** "values are only worth what you're willing to give up for them." (S009)
- **Sources:** 8 (S001, S009, S026, S047, S055, S066, S070, S071)

## 15. Care more, then lockdown *(lockdown is his name)*
- **What it is:** Who ends up building something is usually whoever cares most and keeps going, not whoever has the most resources. Google and Microsoft could have built Facebook and did not. When a rival gets ahead on something strategic, call lockdown: stop other work until the gap is closed.
- **Parts:** Is the rival ahead on something that matters? Then stop everything else (in 2005 the team literally did not leave the house). Do the bigger players care, or is it a side project for them? Internationalize early, because local clones are hard to remove once lodged (S053).
- **When to use:** A founder facing a clone or a fast rival; a founder worried that a giant could enter their market.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the incumbent treats the space as a side project and the founder is all in; 3 if both care; 1 if the founder is the side project.
- **Limits:** Asked whether ignoring the 2005 clone would have changed anything, he said he did not know (S053).
- **Quote:** "we literally did not leave the house until we had addressed the problem" (S053)
- **Sources:** 6 (S013, S052, S053, S064, S065, S075)

## 16. The Year of Efficiency *(his name, 2023)*
- **What it is:** Flatten the org, remove middle layers, cut weak projects sooner, keep engineers at the core, and stay smaller for longer. Five principles: flatter is faster, leaner is better, keep technology the main thing, invest in tools, in-person time helps.
- **Parts:** No manager with more than ten reports, and fill each manager's capacity (S006); count the indirect costs of low-priority projects (leader, space, recruiting, coordination); cut what is not performing; announce restructuring early; hold the theme even if results beat plan (S080).
- **When to use:** A company that hired fast and slowed down; a founder with many layers, many side projects, or an average of three reports per manager (S016).
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the cut removes layers and side bets while protecting the core builders; 3 if it cuts cost without changing structure; 1 if it cuts the people doing the work and keeps the layers.
- **Change over time:** It followed heavy hiring on a pandemic read he got wrong (S048). In August 2022 he called the company forward-leaning on remote work (S013); by March 2023 he pushed in-person time for early-career engineers (S006). He says the cuts were driven by strategy and culture more than money (S016), and that leaner turned out faster (S006, S081).
- **Quote:** "It’s well-understood that every layer of a hierarchy adds latency and risk aversion in information flow and decision-making." (S006)
- **Sources:** 7 (S006, S016, S027, S033, S048, S080, S081)

## 17. Data for the problem, intuition for the solution *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Use data and what users tell you to pick the problem; use intuition to guess the fix; use a rollout to find out. Listen to what people say and watch what they do, and trust behavior when the two differ.
- **Parts:** Qualitative and quantitative listening (S052); stated versus revealed preference (S013); people are poor at predicting their own reaction to new things (S047); first-order wants versus what people value over time (S046).
- **When to use:** A founder citing a survey or customer requests as proof of demand.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder has behavioral data on the problem and a cheap test for the fix; 3 if they have interviews only; 1 if the case rests on what people say they would do.
- **Quote:** "you use data and you use the qualitative feedback that you're getting from from listening to how your community is using your product to tell you what problems to go go solve" (S054)
- **Sources:** 5 (S013, S046, S047, S052, S054)

## 18. The meaningful metric, plus counter metrics *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Measure the version of a number that matters (meaningful groups, not groups; return rates, not launch spikes) and pair every goal metric with counter metrics that track harm. Clicks alone lead to a bad local optimum.
- **Parts:** Retention as daily, weekly, monthly return rates (S051); a meaningful-use filter on the headline number (S085); counter metrics per goal (S026); optimize for what people say is valuable, not time spent (S010); accept lower engagement for a better product, as when he dropped angry reactions as a ranking signal (S013).
- **When to use:** A founder reporting growth, choosing a North Star, or tuning a feed or recommendation system.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the headline metric filters for real use and has a counter metric; 3 if it is a raw count; 1 if it can be gamed by clicks or one-off spikes.
- **Quote:** "we will measure Facebook's progress with groups based on meaningful groups, not groups overall." (S085)
- **Sources:** 7 (S010, S013, S026, S046, S051, S062, S085)

## 19. Define what you are broadly, then jump hills *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Define the company by its mission (human connection) and its technology, not by its current app, so a platform shift is a continuation rather than an identity crisis. Winning makes the next hill hard to see; the hard part is leaving something that works, and being willing to cannibalize it.
- **Parts:** Name what you are beyond the product; check you have the technical competence to deliver it on any platform (S020); notice when you are on a local hill; cannibalize past success to reach the next (S048).
- **When to use:** A founder whose core product is working but whose market is shifting.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder can describe the company without naming the current product and is already building the next one; 3 if they see the shift but are protecting the core; 1 if the company is defined by one app.
- **Quote:** "when you have something that's working well and you're on one local Hill and you need to jump to another Hill that's the stuff that's really culturally hard" (S020)
- **Sources:** 6 (S020, S023, S028, S031, S048, S052)

## 20. Focus: one top priority, and who gets to eat first *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** At scale, focus is scarcer than capital. Only one thing can be the top priority, and he allocates the best people before he allocates money.
- **Parts:** Find the one or two things that matter out of a hundred (S053, a rule he credits to Peter Thiel); keep the main thing the main thing (S018, a line he credits to Ben Horowitz); decide where the best people go first (S022); say no, because big companies that say yes to everything do nothing well (S049).
- **When to use:** A founder running several bets at once or spreading the strongest people across many projects.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder can name the one priority and the best people are on it; 3 if there are two or three co-equal priorities; 1 if everything is a priority.
- **Quote:** "I think about stuff maybe more in terms of people than finances which is like where are you going to allocate the best people and like who gets to eat first" (S022)
- **Sources:** 6 (S018, S022, S023, S049, S053, S055)

## 21. Better and earlier: the tie goes to the incumbent *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** If you arrive on a new platform the same year as the incumbent with a similar product, assume the incumbent wins. So you must be both better and earlier, which means planting seeds years before the platform is defined. Facebook was not born on mobile and could not shape it.
- **Parts:** Who owns the current platform? When will they arrive? Can we be there first and better? Start the seeds once the core is stable (S028).
- **When to use:** A founder entering a category a large company is likely to enter.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the founder will be clearly earlier and better; 3 if earlier but not better; 1 if arriving alongside the incumbent with a similar product.
- **Quote:** "I I kind of assume the tie goes to them" (S022)
- **Sources:** 2 (S022, S028)
